Scheduling Goals & Metrics

A scheduler has no single objective, so you need precise metrics to say what 'better' means for a given workload.

§1The Metrics

MetricDefinitionWho cares
Turnaround timeCompletion time minus arrival timeBatch jobs
Waiting timeTotal time spent in the ready queueEveryone
Response timeTime from arrival to first time on the CPUInteractive users
ThroughputJobs completed per unit timeOperators
CPU utilizationFraction of time the CPU is busyOperators
FairnessEach job gets a reasonable shareMulti-user systems

A job that arrives at time 0, first runs at time 3, and finishes at time 10 has a response time of 3 and a turnaround time of 10.

§2Metrics Conflict

Optimizing one metric usually hurts another. Running short jobs first improves average turnaround, but starves long jobs, which hurts fairness. Switching quickly between jobs improves response time, but each switch wastes CPU time, which lowers throughput.

# Three jobs: A needs 8 units, B needs 4, C needs 2, all arrive at 0.
# Order A,B,C -> turnaround 8, 12, 14  (avg 11.3)
# Order C,B,A -> turnaround 2, 6, 14   (avg 7.3)
# Same total work, very different experience.

§3Preemption

A non-preemptive scheduler lets a job run until it finishes or blocks. A preemptive scheduler can take the CPU away, usually when a timer interrupt fires or a higher-priority job arrives. Modern general-purpose systems are preemptive, because otherwise one runaway loop freezes the machine.

  • CPU-bound jobs use their whole time slice computing
  • I/O-bound jobs run briefly, then block, and want quick response when they wake
  • A good scheduler treats the two kinds differently without being told which is which
The key insight There is no best scheduler, only one that fits a workload and a goal. Always begin by naming the metric you're optimizing: latency, throughput, or fairness.

§4Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Confusing response time with turnaround timeJudging an interactive system by the wrong numberResponse = time to first run; turnaround = time to finish
Optimizing average turnaround aloneLong jobs may starveCheck worst-case and fairness metrics too
Ignoring context-switch cost in calculationsA tiny time slice looks perfect on paper but wastes real CPUInclude switch overhead when picking time slice size
Practice
  1. A job arrives at t=2, first runs at t=5, and finishes at t=12. Compute its turnaround, response, and waiting times (assuming no blocking).
  2. Give one workload where response time matters more than throughput, and one where the opposite is true.
Key takeaways
  • Turnaround, waiting, and response time measure different things.
  • Optimizing one scheduling metric usually degrades another.
  • Preemption protects the system from jobs that never yield.
