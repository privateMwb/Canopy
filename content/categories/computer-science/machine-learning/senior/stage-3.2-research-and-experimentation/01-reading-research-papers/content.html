Reading Research Papers

A reading order that gets you to the useful parts fast: abstract, figures, method, results — in that order, not top to bottom.

§1Why Not Just Read Start to Finish

Papers are written for publication, not for efficient reading. Reading linearly from introduction to conclusion means wading through related-work sections and motivation before finding out if the paper is even relevant to what you need. A better order front-loads the decision of "is this worth my time."

§2Step One: the Abstract

The abstract compresses the entire paper into a few sentences: what problem, what approach, what result. This alone usually answers "is this relevant to what I'm working on" before investing more time.

§3Step Two: the Figures

Skip straight to the figures and their captions next, before reading the method in detail. Figures usually show the architecture diagram and the main results chart — often giving you 80% of the paper's substance in a fraction of the reading time.

§4Step Three: the Method

Only once the abstract and figures confirm relevance is it worth reading the method section closely — this is where you actually understand how the approach works, not just what it claims.

§5Step Four: the Results

Read the results section specifically looking for: what baselines was it compared against (fair ones, or weak strawmen?), how large are the reported improvements, and whether ablations (removing one piece at a time) support the paper's claimed explanation for why it works.

What to skip on a first pass Related work and detailed proofs can usually wait until after you've decided the paper matters. Reading them first is a common way to burn an hour on a paper that turns out not to be relevant.

§6Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Reading start to finish on every paperSlow, and most time spent on papers that turn out irrelevantAbstract → figures → method → results, bail early if not relevant
Trusting reported numbers without checking the baselinesA paper can look impressive against a weak comparisonAlways check what exactly the improvement is measured against
Practice
  1. Pick any ML paper and read only the abstract and figures. Write two sentences on what you think the paper claims.
  2. Then read the results section and check whether the baselines seem fair.
Key takeaways
  • Read abstract → figures → method → results, not start to finish — front-load the relevance decision.
  • Figures often carry most of a paper's substance in a fraction of the reading time.
  • Always check what baselines results are compared against before trusting a reported improvement.
