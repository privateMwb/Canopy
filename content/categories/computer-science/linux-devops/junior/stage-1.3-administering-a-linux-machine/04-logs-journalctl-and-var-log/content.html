Logs: journalctl & /var/log

When a service misbehaves, its logs usually say why, and journalctl plus the files in /var/log are where you look.

§1The systemd Journal

systemd collects the output (stdout and stderr) of every service it runs into the journal, a structured, indexed log. journalctl queries it with filters for unit, time, and priority.

journalctl -u nginx                      # one service
journalctl -u nginx -f                   # follow live
journalctl -u myapp --since "1 hour ago"
journalctl -u myapp -p err               # errors and worse
journalctl -b                            # since this boot
journalctl -b -1                         # previous boot
journalctl -k                            # kernel messages

Priorities run from emerg (0) down to debug (7). -p err shows err and anything more severe.

§2Classic Log Files

Many programs still write their own files under /var/log: auth.log (or secure) for logins, syslog or messages for general events, and per-application directories such as nginx/access.log and nginx/error.log. The earlier text tools work directly on them.

sudo tail -f /var/log/nginx/error.log
sudo grep "Failed password" /var/log/auth.log | tail
sudo awk '{print $9}' /var/log/nginx/access.log | sort | uniq -c   # status codes
dmesg -T | tail                          # kernel ring buffer with timestamps

§3Rotation and Disk Space

Logs grow forever unless something rotates them. logrotate renames, compresses, and prunes old files on a schedule. The journal has its own size limits, and a full disk is one of the most common causes of mysterious failures.

cat /etc/logrotate.d/nginx               # see a rotation policy
journalctl --disk-usage
sudo journalctl --vacuum-size=500M       # shrink the journal
df -h /var                               # is the log partition full?
Log to stdout in modern setups Under systemd and in containers, the best practice is for an app to write plain lines to stdout and stderr and let the platform collect them. That keeps rotation, shipping, and retention out of your application code.

§4Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Reading only the last error lineThe real cause is often several lines earlierRead the surrounding context with -n or --since
Ignoring disk spaceA full /var makes services fail in strange waysCheck df -h early in any investigation
Not rotating custom log filesOne file grows until the disk fillsAdd a logrotate rule or log to the journal
Practice
  1. Find every error logged by one service in the last hour, and count them.
  2. Intentionally fail a login over SSH and locate the matching entry in the auth log.
Key takeaways
  • journalctl -u <unit> -f follows a service's logs, and -p, --since, and -b narrow them.
  • Traditional logs live in /var/log and work with grep, awk, and tail.
  • Rotate logs and watch disk usage, since a full disk breaks everything.
