SLIs, SLOs & Error Budgets

An SLO turns "reliable enough" into a number, and the error budget it leaves tells a team when to ship and when to stop and fix.

§1SLIs and SLOs

A service level indicator (SLI) is a measurement of user-visible quality, such as the fraction of requests that succeed or finish under 300 ms. A service level objective (SLO) is the target for it over a window, for example "99.9 percent of requests succeed over 30 days". An SLA is the contractual promise with consequences, and it should be looser than your internal SLO.

# SLI: good events / valid events
sum(rate(http_requests_total{status!~"5.."}[30d]))
  / sum(rate(http_requests_total[30d]))
# SLO:  >= 99.9% over a rolling 30 days

Pick SLIs users actually feel. CPU usage is not one, but a successful, fast checkout is.

§2The Error Budget

If the SLO is 99.9 percent, then 0.1 percent of requests are allowed to fail. That allowance is the error budget: about 43 minutes of full downtime in 30 days. It is not a failure target. It is permission to take risks, since reliability work costs feature velocity and perfection is unaffordable.

# allowed downtime in a 30-day window (43,200 minutes)
# 99%      -> 432 min  (about 7.2 hours)
# 99.9%    ->  43.2 min
# 99.99%   ->   4.3 min
# each extra nine costs far more engineering and money than the last

§3Using the Budget to Decide

Write an error budget policy before you need it. While budget remains, ship at normal speed. When it is nearly spent, slow down: prioritize reliability fixes, freeze risky launches, and review what consumed it. This replaces opinion fights between "ship faster" and "be safer" with a shared number.

# example policy
#   budget > 50% left   -> normal release cadence
#   budget < 25% left   -> only reliability work and low-risk changes
#   budget exhausted    -> feature freeze until back within SLO
#   one incident > 50% of the budget -> mandatory postmortem
100 percent is the wrong target Chasing perfect reliability is slow, expensive, and unnecessary, since users' own networks and devices fail more often than that. The right SLO is the lowest level at which users stay happy, because every extra nine is bought with speed.

§4Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Choosing SLIs that are easy rather than meaningfulDashboards look green while users sufferMeasure what users experience, such as successful and fast requests
Setting the SLO at 100 percentNo budget for change, so the team stops shippingChoose a realistic target, such as 99.9
Writing the policy during an incidentThe decision becomes political and rushedAgree on it in advance with product and engineering
Practice
  1. Define an SLI and SLO for one real service, and compute its monthly error budget in minutes.
  2. Write a one-page error budget policy that product and engineering could both sign.
Key takeaways
  • An SLI measures user-visible quality, and an SLO is the target for it.
  • The error budget is the allowed unreliability, and it is permission to take risks.
  • A pre-agreed policy turns the budget into an objective ship-or-fix decision.
