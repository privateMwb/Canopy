Post-Quantum Cryptography & Crypto Agility

Large quantum computers would break today's public-key cryptography but not symmetric cryptography, and the migration has to start before such machines exist, because encrypted data can be recorded now.

§1What Is and Isn't at Risk

Algorithm typeEffect of a large quantum computerStatus
RSA, Diffie-Hellman, elliptic curves (key exchange and signatures)Broken by Shor's algorithmMust be replaced
AES, ChaCha20, and similar symmetric ciphersGrover's algorithm gives only a quadratic speedupUse 256-bit keys for comfortable margin
SHA-2, SHA-3 hashesSame quadratic effect on preimage searchUse 256-bit or larger outputs

Such machines don't exist yet at the required scale. The urgent threat is "harvest now, decrypt later": an adversary records encrypted traffic today and decrypts it once a quantum computer exists. That matters for data that must stay secret for many years. Signatures matter on a different clock: they need replacing before forgery becomes feasible, especially long-lived roots and firmware keys.

§2The New Standards

In August 2024, NIST published the first post-quantum standards:

  • ML-KEM (FIPS 203, derived from CRYSTALS-Kyber): key establishment.
  • ML-DSA (FIPS 204, from CRYSTALS-Dilithium): digital signatures.
  • SLH-DSA (FIPS 205, from SPHINCS+): a hash-based signature scheme with conservative assumptions.

Costs are real: ML-KEM-768 public keys and ciphertexts are around a kilobyte each, and ML-DSA signatures are several kilobytes, much larger than their elliptic-curve counterparts. That affects handshake size, certificates, and constrained devices.

§3Hybrid Deployment and Crypto Agility

Because these algorithms are newer, deployments commonly use a hybrid key exchange, such as X25519 combined with ML-KEM-768 in TLS: the session stays secure if *either* component holds. Major browsers and CDNs have begun deploying this.

  • Inventory where you use public-key cryptography (a cryptographic bill of materials): TLS, VPNs, code signing, tokens, stored data.
  • Don't hard-code algorithms. Put an algorithm identifier and version in every key, token, and ciphertext format so you can change them.
  • Prioritize long-lived confidentiality and long-lived signing keys first.
  • Test migrations for larger sizes and performance before they're urgent, and follow current guidance, since timelines and recommendations are still evolving.
Agility is the lasting lesson Whatever happens with quantum computers, algorithms get weakened over time (MD5, SHA-1, 1024-bit RSA). Systems that can swap algorithms without a rewrite are the real goal.

§4Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Waiting until quantum computers exist to planRecorded traffic is already exposed to harvest-now attacksInventory and prioritize long-lived secrets now
Assuming symmetric cryptography must be replacedEffort is wasted where the risk is smallMove to 256-bit keys and focus on public-key replacement
Hard-coding algorithm choices in formatsMigration needs a breaking changeInclude algorithm IDs and versions in every format
Practice
  1. List every place your system uses RSA or elliptic-curve cryptography, and mark which protect data that must stay secret for 10 years.
  2. Add an algorithm identifier and version field to a token or ciphertext format you own.
Key takeaways
  • Shor's algorithm threatens RSA and elliptic-curve cryptography; symmetric cryptography needs only larger keys.
  • Harvest-now-decrypt-later makes long-lived confidential data the first priority.
  • Use hybrid key exchange and build crypto agility with inventories and versioned formats.
