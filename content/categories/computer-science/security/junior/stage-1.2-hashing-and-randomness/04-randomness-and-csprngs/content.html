Randomness & CSPRNGs

Tokens, keys, and password-reset links are only secure if an attacker can't guess them, and that depends entirely on where your randomness comes from.

§1Predictable Versus Unpredictable

Ordinary random number generators, such as Math.random() or Python's random module, are built for speed and statistical spread. Their output follows a pattern from an internal state, and after seeing a few values an attacker can often predict the rest. They are fine for shuffling a playlist and never for secrets.

A cryptographically secure pseudo-random number generator (CSPRNG) is designed so that past output reveals nothing about future output, even to someone who knows the algorithm.

§2Where Secure Randomness Comes From

The operating system collects unpredictable events (timings, hardware noise) into an entropy pool and exposes a CSPRNG over it. Use the OS source through your language's secure API.

§3How Much Is Enough

Length is measured in bits of entropy. A guess space of 2^128 is beyond brute force for any attacker, so 128 bits is the usual floor for session IDs and reset tokens, and 256 is common for keys. Remember that a 128-bit token is 16 random bytes, regardless of how you encode it.

  • Session IDs, reset links, API keys, CSRF tokens, and encryption keys all need a CSPRNG.
  • Never derive secrets from timestamps, user IDs, or counters, even if they "look random."
  • Don't seed a CSPRNG yourself with a fixed or guessable value.
Why this fails in the real world Predictable "random" tokens have repeatedly let attackers take over accounts by guessing the next reset link. Stage 3.2 shows how weak randomness has broken real cryptographic systems.

§4Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Using Math.random() or random for tokensOutputs can be predicted from a few samplesUse the CSPRNG from the OS-backed secure API
Building tokens from time or user IDGuessable by anyone who knows the patternGenerate pure random bytes
Too-short tokensBrute-force becomes feasibleUse at least 128 bits for tokens and 256 for keys
Practice
  1. Generate a 256-bit token in your language of choice using its secure API, and write down how many bytes that is.
  2. Find one place in an app you've built that uses non-secure randomness for something an attacker would want to guess.
Key takeaways
  • Ordinary random functions are predictable and unsafe for secrets.
  • Use the CSPRNG that your OS and language expose for tokens and keys.
  • Use at least 128 bits of randomness for tokens, and never derive secrets from time or IDs.
