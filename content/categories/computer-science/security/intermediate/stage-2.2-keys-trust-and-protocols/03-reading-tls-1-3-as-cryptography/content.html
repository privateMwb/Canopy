Reading TLS 1.3 as Cryptography

Now that you know the primitives, TLS 1.3 reads as a short recipe: a key exchange, a signature, a key derivation, a MAC, and an AEAD, each protecting one specific thing.

§1The Recipe, Primitive by Primitive

The Networking track walked through the handshake as a protocol. Here is the same handshake as the cryptography you just learned.

StepPrimitiveWhat it protects
Both sides send ephemeral public keysX25519 (or another ECDH group)Agreement on a shared secret nobody on the wire can compute
Secret is turned into traffic keysHKDF over the secret and a transcript hashSeparate keys per purpose and direction
Server proves its identitySignature (CertificateVerify) over the handshake transcriptThat the party holding the certificate's private key took part
Certificate links name to keyCertificate chain of signaturesThat the key belongs to that hostname
Each side confirms the handshakeMAC (Finished) over the transcriptThat neither side's handshake messages were altered
Application dataAEAD (AES-GCM or ChaCha20-Poly1305), nonce from a record counterConfidentiality and integrity of every record

§2Why the Transcript Matters

Both the signature and the Finished MAC cover a hash of the whole handshake so far. That binds the keys to exactly what was negotiated, so an attacker who tries to downgrade the version or swap parameters changes the transcript, and verification fails. It's the "include context in what you authenticate" lesson from MACs, applied to a protocol.

§3Where Each Guarantee Lives

  • Forward secrecy comes from the ephemeral key exchange. The certificate's key only signs, so stealing it later doesn't decrypt recorded traffic.
  • Authentication comes from the signature plus the certificate chain, not from encryption.
  • Integrity comes from the AEAD tags and the Finished MACs.
  • Nonce safety comes from a per-record sequence number combined with an IV, so no nonce repeats within a connection.
Reading a protocol When you meet any security protocol, ask of each message: which primitive protects it, what is it bound to, and what would an attacker gain by altering it. TLS 1.3 is a good worked example.

§4Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Thinking the certificate key encrypts the trafficA wrong picture of what a key compromise exposesIt signs the handshake; session keys come from the ephemeral exchange
Treating authentication and encryption as one thingEncrypted but unauthenticated channels are open to a man-in-the-middleCheck both: who is on the other end, and who can read and alter the data
Ignoring the transcript bindingDowngrade and parameter-swap attacks look harmlessAuthenticate the negotiation itself, not just the data
Practice
  1. Capture a TLS 1.3 handshake in Wireshark and label the messages that carry a key share, a certificate, a signature, and a Finished.
  2. Explain in three sentences how the transcript hash defeats a downgrade attack.
Key takeaways
  • TLS 1.3 combines ECDH, HKDF, signatures, MACs, and AEAD, each guarding a specific property.
  • Signatures and Finished messages cover the whole handshake transcript.
  • Forward secrecy, authentication, and integrity come from different primitives.
