Explaining Hardware Tradeoffs

Choosing a machine, or defending a performance decision, means turning hardware facts into a recommendation that engineers and decision-makers can both act on.

§1Characterize the Workload First

Specifications don't tell you which machine is best, but the workload does. Before comparing options, measure what limits it.

If the workload is...Then it needs...Look at
Compute-bound (high IPC, few misses)Clock speed, wide cores, SIMDIPC, vector use, core count
Memory-bandwidth-boundMemory channels and bandwidth per coreSTREAM results, DRAM-bound %
Latency-bound (pointer chasing)Low memory latency, big cachesL3 size, DRAM latency
I/O-boundFast storage or network, not more coresDevice utilization, queue depth
Highly parallel mathGPU or accelerator, and a fast linkArithmetic intensity, transfer cost

Gather this evidence with the tools from earlier lessons: perf stat, top-down analysis, and bandwidth and latency benchmarks, run on representative data.

§2Compare on Cost per Unit of Work

A bigger machine is not automatically better value. Convert each option to cost per unit of work under your own benchmark.

# Illustrative numbers from a load test of the same service:
Option X:   $0.40 / hour,   900 requests/s  ->  3.24 M requests/hour  ->  $0.123 per million
Option Y:   $0.55 / hour, 1,500 requests/s  ->  5.40 M requests/hour  ->  $0.102 per million
# Y costs more per hour but less per request. Check latency percentiles too, not just throughput.
  • vCPUs are usually hardware threads (SMT), so 8 vCPUs may be 4 physical cores
  • Memory bandwidth per core often falls as cores increase, so a many-core part can disappoint a bandwidth-bound job
  • NUMA and sockets affect latency and scaling
  • Power and cooling are real costs in owned hardware, and efficiency matters for sustainability goals
  • Headroom and failure: include the cost of running below peak utilization for reliability

§3Writing and Presenting the Decision

A clear write-up has a standard shape.

  1. Workload: what runs, how it's measured, and what limits it, with evidence
  2. Requirements and constraints: latency targets, throughput, budget, availability, and compliance
  3. Options considered: the realistic alternatives, with measured results, not spec-sheet claims
  4. Recommendation: the choice and the main reason, with numbers
  5. Risks and assumptions: what could make it wrong, such as growth, a change in access pattern, or a vendor change
  6. Revisit triggers: the measurable conditions under which you would reconsider

Adapt it to the audience. Engineers want counters, benchmark methodology, and failure modes. Business stakeholders want cost per unit of work, latency users will feel, and risk, in plain terms: 'Option Y costs 17% more per hour but handles 67% more traffic, which lowers our cost per request by 17%.' Name the assumptions, and say what you measured and what you estimated.

The most defensible answer is often a measured one. When someone proposes a different machine, the strongest reply is not an argument but a benchmark of their option on the same workload.

The key insight Hardware knowledge becomes judgment when you can say what limits the workload, what each option costs per unit of work, and what would change your mind.

§4Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Choosing by spec-sheet numbersPeak specs don't predict your workloadBenchmark candidates on representative data
Comparing hourly price instead of cost per requestA pricier machine can be cheaper per unit of workNormalize by measured throughput at your latency target
Presenting a recommendation without assumptionsThe reasoning can't be checked or revisitedState assumptions and the conditions that would change the decision
Practice
  1. Take a service you know and classify it as compute-, bandwidth-, latency-, or I/O-bound, citing the measurement that shows it.
  2. Write a one-page recommendation comparing two machine types for that service, including cost per unit of work and revisit triggers.
Key takeaways
  • Characterize the workload's limiting resource before comparing hardware.
  • Compare options by measured cost per unit of work at the required latency, and account for SMT and bandwidth per core.
  • Write decisions with workload, options, recommendation, risks, and revisit triggers, tailored to the audience.
