FPGAs, ASICs & the Efficiency Tradeoff

The more a chip is specialized to one task, the less energy it wastes on flexibility, and the more the specialization has to be justified by volume, stability, and need.

§1A Spectrum From General to Specific

CPUGPUFPGAASIC
FlexibilityHighestHigh for parallel workReconfigurableNone after fabrication
Efficiency (work per watt)BaselineHigher on parallel tasksHigher on tuned tasksHighest for its task
Development effortLow (software)Moderate (kernels)High (hardware design)Very high

Each step to the right removes general-purpose overhead, such as instruction fetch and decode, speculation, and large caches, and spends the savings on circuits shaped to the task. For a fixed workload, an ASIC can be one to several orders of magnitude more energy-efficient than a CPU.

§2FPGAs

A field-programmable gate array is a chip of configurable logic: lookup tables (LUTs) that implement arbitrary small functions, flip-flops, programmable wiring, and hardened blocks such as DSP multipliers and block RAM. You describe a circuit in a hardware description language (Verilog, VHDL) or with high-level synthesis, and the chip is configured to be that circuit.

  • Strengths: deterministic low latency, custom data paths and bit widths, direct connection to network and sensor I/O, and reconfiguration after deployment
  • Weaknesses: clocks of a few hundred MHz, much lower than CPUs, harder development and debugging, and a lower density and efficiency than an ASIC
  • Typical uses: network and packet processing, financial trading, signal processing, prototyping ASICs, and low-volume specialized products

§3ASICs and the Economics

An application-specific integrated circuit is fixed at manufacture. The one-time non-recurring engineering (NRE) cost, for design, verification, and mask sets, can reach tens to hundreds of millions of dollars at leading-edge process nodes, so an ASIC makes sense only for very large volumes, or for a use where efficiency is worth almost any price.

  • Examples: video codecs in every phone, Bitcoin miners, Google's TPUs, neural-processing units in phones and laptops, and smart NICs
  • Risk: if the algorithm changes, a fixed design can become obsolete. This is why accelerators for fast-moving fields keep some programmability
  • Ecosystem: hardware is only as useful as its software. A strong toolchain, like CUDA for GPUs, can matter as much as the silicon
# A rough decision flow:
stable, huge volume, energy or latency critical ......... ASIC (or accelerator block in an SoC)
parallel math, evolving algorithms ...................... GPU or matrix accelerator
custom low-latency pipeline, moderate volume ............ FPGA
everything else ......................................... CPU

Amdahl's law applies at the system level. Accelerating 80% of a workload by 100× gives only about 4.8× overall, so the remaining 20% on the CPU, plus data movement, can decide whether a specialized chip is worth building.

The key insight Specialization trades flexibility for efficiency, and the trade only pays when the workload is stable, large, and well-understood. Many systems now combine a general core with accelerators for the few tasks that dominate.

§4Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Choosing an ASIC for a workload that is still changingThe design is outdated before it pays backKeep programmability, or use GPUs and FPGAs until the algorithm stabilizes
Judging an accelerator by peak numbers aloneReal workloads don't reach peak, and data movement mattersMeasure end-to-end on representative data
Ignoring software toolingA fast chip nobody can program wastes the siliconWeigh the compilers, libraries, and debugging tools available
Practice
  1. Pick a task such as video decoding, packet filtering, or model training, and argue which of CPU, GPU, FPGA, or ASIC fits best, citing volume and stability.
  2. A workload is 90% accelerable by 50×. What overall speedup results? What if data transfer adds 10% of the original runtime?
Key takeaways
  • Efficiency rises, and flexibility falls, from CPU to GPU to FPGA to ASIC.
  • FPGAs offer reconfigurable custom logic, and ASICs need huge volume to repay their upfront cost.
  • System-level speedup is bounded by Amdahl's law, the software ecosystem, and data movement.
