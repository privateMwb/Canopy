Designing a Team Git Policy

A written Git policy turns good habits into shared defaults, and its quality depends on what it leaves out.

§1What a Policy Should Cover

  • Branching and merging: model, naming, who may merge, squash or merge commits.
  • Review: required approvals, who reviews what, expected response time, size guidance.
  • Commits: message format, signing, atomic changes.
  • CI and protection: required checks, merge queue, up-to-date rule.
  • Releases: versioning, tags, hotfix procedure, changelog.
  • Security: secrets handling, access reviews, signing, incident steps.
  • History rewriting: what is allowed and where.

§2Keep It Short and Enforced

A policy no one reads does nothing. Aim for one or two pages, put the rules in tooling (branch protection, hooks, templates, CI), and link to examples.

# .github/pull_request_template.md
## What and why
## How I tested
## Risk and rollback

§3Exceptions

Allow documented exceptions with an owner, such as a legacy repository that cannot require signed commits. Review exceptions regularly.

§4Rolling It Out

Start with the three rules that prevent the most pain, enforce them, collect feedback, and add more later. Announce changes, explain the reason for each rule, and migrate repositories gradually.

§5Measuring Whether It Works

Track branch lifetime, time to review, time to merge, failed main builds, and revert rate. If a rule does not move a number or prevent a known problem, remove it.

The key insight A policy is a product for your teammates. If it is long, unexplained, and unenforced, it will be ignored.

§6Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Copying another company's policy unchangedRules do not match your teamStart from your actual problems
Rules without enforcementCompliance varies by personEncode rules in branch protection and CI
Never revisiting the policyRules outlive their reasonsReview it each quarter and remove what no longer helps
Practice
  1. Draft a one-page Git policy for a team you know, with at least three enforced rules and two exceptions.
  2. Pick two metrics and describe how you would measure whether the policy improved them.
Key takeaways
  • A good policy is short, explained, and enforced in tooling.
  • Allow documented exceptions and review them.
  • Measure results and remove rules that do not help.
