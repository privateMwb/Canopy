Is Kubernetes the Right Tool?

Kubernetes is a powerful answer to a specific set of problems, and the senior skill is recognizing when it is the wrong answer.

§1What You Are Really Buying

Kubernetes provides a portable, extensible platform for running many containerized services with scheduling, self-healing, service discovery, declarative rollout, and an ecosystem of tools. You pay in complexity: concepts to learn, components to run, YAML to maintain, security to configure, upgrades to schedule, and engineers who understand it.

A useful framing is total cost of ownership: infrastructure cost, plus engineer time to run, plus time developers spend learning, minus the time saved by the platform's capabilities.

§2Signs It Is Worth It

  • Many services (dozens or more) with varied scaling needs and independent teams and release cadences.
  • A need for portability across clouds, on-premises, or edge, or for a consistent platform across them.
  • Substantial in-house capability, or a budget for a platform team or managed services.
  • Workloads that benefit from bin packing, autoscaling, and rich scheduling, such as batch, ML, or mixed-size services.
  • The ecosystem itself is valuable: operators, GitOps, policy engines, and service meshes.

§3Signs It Is Not

  • A handful of services, or a monolith with a database.
  • A tiny team with no time to learn or operate a platform.
  • Steady, predictable load that does not need dynamic scheduling.
  • The motive is fashion, a resume, or "everyone uses it."

Many of these run containers and give you rolling deploys, health checks, and autoscaling with far less machinery.

§5Managed or Self-Run

If Kubernetes is the right fit, use a managed control plane (EKS, GKE, AKS) unless a requirement prevents it. Consider GKE Autopilot or similar modes if you want even less node management. Self-run clusters make sense on-premises, in constrained environments, or at a scale where the control is worth the staffing.

§6Reversibility

Start with the simplest thing that meets today's needs while avoiding choices that trap you: containerize applications, keep configuration in the environment, and use standard interfaces. A container that runs on Cloud Run today can move to Kubernetes later, but a company-wide Kubernetes platform is much harder to unwind.

Complexity is a loan Kubernetes can repay the loan many times over at the right scale. At the wrong one you pay interest, in engineer-hours, every month.

§7Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Adopting Kubernetes first and deriving requirements laterThe platform's cost exceeds its benefitList requirements and compare options before choosing
Ignoring simpler managed optionsYou operate infrastructure that a service could run for youEvaluate PaaS, serverless containers, and ECS honestly
Underestimating day-two operationsUpgrades, security, and incidents consume the teamInclude operations staffing in the decision
Practice
  1. For a system you know, score it against the "worth it" and "not worth it" lists and give a recommendation.
  2. Choose two alternatives and describe what you would give up by not using Kubernetes.
Key takeaways
  • Kubernetes buys flexibility and an ecosystem and costs significant complexity.
  • Many small and medium systems are better served by simpler platforms.
  • If you choose it, prefer managed control planes and plan for day-two operations.
