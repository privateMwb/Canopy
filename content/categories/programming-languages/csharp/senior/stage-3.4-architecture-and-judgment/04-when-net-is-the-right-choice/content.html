When .NET Is the Right Choice

Choosing a platform is an engineering decision with tradeoffs. A senior engineer can state them honestly and argue either side.

§1Where .NET Excels

  • Productive, safe general-purpose language: C# is statically typed with a strong type system, nullable analysis, pattern matching, and one of the best async models in mainstream use.
  • Performance with a managed runtime: a modern JIT with dynamic PGO, spans and low-allocation APIs, and a tuned garbage collector. ASP.NET Core ranks highly in independent web-framework benchmarks.
  • Batteries included: one coherent stack for web, data, DI, configuration, logging, and testing, maintained and versioned together.
  • Tooling: excellent IDEs (Visual Studio, Rider, VS Code), debuggers, profilers, and the diagnostics tools from earlier lessons.
  • Breadth: web APIs, background services, desktop (WPF, WinForms, MAUI), games (Unity uses C#), cloud functions, CLI tools, and cross-platform deployment on Windows, Linux, and macOS.
  • Enterprise fit and longevity: strong backward-compatibility culture, a predictable release cadence, long-term support, and a large hiring pool.

§2Where It Costs More Than It's Worth

  • Hard real-time or tight memory control: a garbage collector means pauses and less predictable memory, which C, C++, or Rust handle with more control (at more engineering cost).
  • Very small, fast-starting functions: the JIT and runtime add startup and memory overhead. Native AOT helps, but Go or Rust binaries are still smaller and simpler for tiny tools.
  • Machine-learning research and data science: the ecosystem and community center on Python. .NET has ML.NET and bindings, but you'll swim upstream.
  • Ecosystem gaps: some domains (certain scientific libraries, some cloud SDK features that appear first elsewhere) have fewer or later libraries.
  • Front-end web: Blazor works, but the JavaScript and TypeScript ecosystem dominates, so hiring and libraries lean that way.
  • Perception and team skills: a team of Go or Python experts will be slower in C# at first, and hiring pools differ by region and industry.

§3Arguing Either Way

Start from the constraints instead of the language. Ask:

  1. What are the hard requirements: latency, memory, startup time, platforms, compliance, existing systems to integrate with?
  2. What does the team know today, and what can you hire?
  3. What does the organization already operate: pipelines, monitoring, security reviews, cloud accounts?
  4. What is the lifetime: a prototype for a quarter or a system for ten years?
  5. What is the cost of being wrong, and how easy is it to change course later?

Then argue both sides, as in a good decision record. For: "We have eight engineers who know C#, our Azure and SQL Server estate integrates natively, and ASP.NET Core meets the latency target with headroom." Against: "The workload is a tiny event-processing function where cold start dominates, and the team has Go experience, so Go is likely the cheaper choice." A decision you can defend states the criteria, weighs the evidence, and names the risks accepted. Your preference for a language isn't an argument.

The key insight "Right" is relative to constraints, team, and lifetime. Say what you'd measure to change your mind, and you've made an engineering decision instead of a loyalty statement.

§4Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Choosing a platform by benchmark headlinesReal performance depends on design, I/O, and workload, not language alonePrototype your actual workload and measure
Ignoring team skills and operational realitySlow delivery and unfamiliar failures in productionCount the cost of learning, hiring, and operating in the decision
Treating the choice as permanent or purely technicalOverinvestment in analysis or defending a sunk costDecide with the information you have, record the reasons, and revisit when facts change
Practice
  1. Pick a real project idea and write a half-page recommendation for or against .NET, listing the criteria and the evidence you'd gather.
  2. Write the strongest argument for a language you would not choose, then answer it.
Key takeaways
  • The platform shines for productive, safe, performant general-purpose services with excellent tooling and a coherent stack.
  • It costs more for hard real-time work, tiny fast-start tools, ML research, and ecosystems centered elsewhere.
  • Decide from constraints, team, lifetime, and cost of being wrong, and be able to argue both sides.
