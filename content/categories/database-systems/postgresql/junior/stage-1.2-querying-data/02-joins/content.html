Joins

A join combines rows from two tables on a condition, and the join type decides what happens to rows that have no match.

§1INNER and LEFT Joins

Relational data is split across tables, usually linked by a key. books.author_id points at authors.id, and a join follows that link.

SELECT b.title, a.name
FROM books b
JOIN authors a ON a.id = b.author_id;            -- INNER JOIN (the default)

SELECT a.name, b.title
FROM authors a
LEFT JOIN books b ON b.author_id = a.id;         -- keeps authors with no books

An inner join returns only rows with a match on both sides. A left join returns every row from the left table and fills the right side's columns with NULL where nothing matched. RIGHT JOIN is the mirror image and is rarely needed because you can swap the table order.

Short table aliases (b, a) keep queries readable, and qualifying every column with its alias avoids ambiguity errors.

§2FULL, CROSS, and Self Joins

  • FULL JOIN keeps unmatched rows from both sides; useful for reconciliation ("what is in A but not B, and vice versa").
  • CROSS JOIN pairs every row with every row (a Cartesian product): 100 rows times 1,000 rows is 100,000 rows. Useful for generating combinations, dangerous by accident.
  • Self join: join a table to itself using two aliases, for example employees and their managers.
SELECT e.name AS employee, m.name AS manager
FROM employees e
LEFT JOIN employees m ON m.id = e.manager_id;

SELECT a.name
FROM authors a
LEFT JOIN books b ON b.author_id = a.id
WHERE b.id IS NULL;                -- anti-join: authors with no books

The last query is the standard anti-join pattern: left join, then keep rows where the right side is NULL. NOT EXISTS expresses the same idea and is often just as fast.

§3Choosing the Right Join

Ask what should happen to a row with no partner.

QuestionJoin
Only things that have bothINNER JOIN
Everything on the left, partner if anyLEFT JOIN
Left rows that have no partnerLEFT JOIN ... WHERE right.id IS NULL
All differences either wayFULL JOIN

A frequent trap: put a filter on the right table of a LEFT JOIN in WHERE, and the NULL rows are filtered out, silently turning it into an inner join. Put the condition in the ON clause instead.

SELECT a.name, count(b.id)
FROM authors a
LEFT JOIN books b ON b.author_id = a.id AND b.in_stock   -- filter in ON
GROUP BY a.name;
The key insight Decide the join type by the unmatched rows. If you do not know what should happen to a row with no match, you do not know which join you need.

§4Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Filtering the right table of a LEFT JOIN in WHEREUnmatched rows disappear; the join behaves like an inner joinMove the condition into the ON clause
Joining without a condition or with the wrong oneA Cartesian product multiplies row counts and hides bugsCheck row counts; write every ON clause deliberately
Joining on a non-unique column and expecting one row per left rowRows are duplicated, inflating sums and countsJoin on the unique side, or aggregate before joining
Practice
  1. List every author and their number of books, including authors with zero books.
  2. Find all customers who have never placed an order using an anti-join.
  3. Show how a WHERE b.in_stock after a LEFT JOIN changes the result compared with putting it in ON.
Key takeaways
  • Inner joins drop unmatched rows; left joins keep them with NULLs on the other side.
  • The anti-join (LEFT JOIN ... WHERE right.id IS NULL) finds rows with no partner.
  • Filters on the nullable side of an outer join belong in ON.
