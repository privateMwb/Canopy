When Postgres Is the Wrong Tool

Postgres can do a great deal, and part of seniority is knowing precisely where it stops and what another system would give you.

§1What Postgres Does Surprisingly Well

Before adding another system, notice how far one Postgres goes: relational data, jsonb documents, full-text search, geospatial queries, vector similarity, a job queue with SKIP LOCKED, pub/sub with LISTEN/NOTIFY, time-series with partitioning, and moderate analytics. Using one database avoids synchronization pipelines, gives you transactional consistency across all of it, and reduces what the team must operate and understand.

That is a strong default: start with Postgres, and add a system only for a measured reason. Every additional datastore adds deployment, monitoring, backup, security, upgrade, and failure-mode costs, and creates the problem of keeping two sources of truth consistent.

§2Honest Limits

Postgres has real boundaries:

  • A single writer. All writes go through one primary. Vertical scaling takes you far, but very high, sustained write rates or datasets needing horizontal sharding go beyond a single node. Sharding is possible (application-level, or extensions such as Citus) but adds substantial complexity.
  • Multi-region active-active writes are not built in; distributed SQL systems are designed for that.
  • Large-scale analytics. Row storage and a transactional design make multi-terabyte scans slower and costlier than in columnar warehouses.
  • Sub-millisecond caching at huge request rates is better served by an in-memory store.
  • Very high-throughput event streaming and fan-out fits a log-based broker better than a table.
  • Search relevance, typo tolerance, and faceting at scale are the strength of dedicated search engines.
  • Deep graph traversals over billions of edges suit a graph database.
  • Large blobs (videos, backups) belong in object storage, with Postgres holding references.
  • Embedded, offline, or single-file use is what SQLite is for.

Also remember operational realities: high availability requires tooling you assemble or buy, vacuum and bloat need care, and the connection model needs pooling.

§3Deciding with Evidence

Use a small, honest process whenever someone proposes another datastore.

  1. State the requirement in numbers: throughput, latency, data size, consistency needs, availability target.
  2. Measure Postgres against it with a realistic workload, tuned with the techniques in this track. Often the answer is an index, a replica, or partitioning.
  3. Count the full cost of the alternative: operations, on-call, data synchronization, consistency bugs, training, and vendor risk.
  4. Choose the boundary carefully if you do add a system: which one is the source of truth, how changes flow (CDC, outbox pattern), and how you recover if they disagree.
  5. Write it down as a decision record, including the evidence and what would make you revisit it.
-- A pattern for keeping a second system consistent: the transactional outbox
BEGIN;
INSERT INTO orders (...) VALUES (...);
INSERT INTO outbox (aggregate, event, payload) VALUES ('order', 'created', '{...}');
COMMIT;      -- a separate process reads outbox and publishes to the other system

Defending Postgres where it fits, and saying clearly where it does not, is exactly the judgment this level of the track is about.

The key insight The right question is not "can Postgres do this?" but "what does the requirement measure, and what does each option cost to run?" Boring, well-understood infrastructure is a feature.

§4Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Adding a new datastore for a need Postgres already meetsExtra operations, sync bugs, and two sources of truthMeasure Postgres first; add a system only for a measured gap
Staying on Postgres out of loyalty after a real limit is reachedHeroic workarounds and growing operational riskState the requirement in numbers and choose the tool that meets it
Dual-writing to two systems without a consistency planSilent divergenceUse one source of truth plus an outbox or CDC to feed the other
Practice
  1. Take a proposed use of another datastore (cache, search, queue, or warehouse) and write the requirement in numbers, then test whether tuned Postgres meets it.
  2. Describe how a transactional outbox keeps Postgres and a message broker consistent, and what happens when the publisher crashes.
  3. List three workloads from your experience where you would not choose Postgres, and name the better fit.
Key takeaways
  • Postgres covers relational, JSON, search, geospatial, vector, queueing, and moderate analytics workloads in one transactional system.
  • Its limits: a single writer, no built-in sharding or multi-region writes, columnar-scale analytics, and extreme caching or streaming workloads.
  • Decide with measurements and total cost, keep a single source of truth, and document the decision.
