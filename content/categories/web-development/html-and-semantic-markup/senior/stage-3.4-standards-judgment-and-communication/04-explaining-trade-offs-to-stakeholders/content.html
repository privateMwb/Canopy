Explaining Trade-offs to Stakeholders

Persuading non-engineers to invest in semantics and quality means translating markup decisions into risk, cost, and user outcomes they care about.

§1Speak Their Language

Stakeholders rarely care about <button> versus <div>. They care about revenue, risk, cost, reputation, and delivery dates. Your job is to connect the technical choice to one of those.

Technical pointStakeholder framing
Semantic, accessible markupMore customers can complete purchases; lower legal exposure; fewer support tickets
Native elements over custom widgetsFaster delivery now and lower maintenance costs later
Streaming and lean HTMLFaster load times, which correlates with conversion and search visibility
Sanitizing user HTMLPrevents account takeovers and data breaches
Design system contractsLess rework between teams; consistent brand

Use credible numbers where you have them: percentage of users with disabilities, your own checkout abandonment, or the cost of the last incident. Avoid inflated statistics you can't source.

§2Present Options, Not Ultimatums

Give a recommendation along with alternatives, costs, and what each one risks. This builds trust and lets the decision-maker choose with open eyes.

Decision: How do we build the new date picker?

Option A  Native <input type="date">, styled
          Cost: 2 days. Risk: limited visual customization.
Option B  Open-source accessible component
          Cost: 1 week. Risk: dependency upkeep.
Option C  Custom build
          Cost: 4–6 weeks + ongoing. Risk: accessibility regressions.

Recommendation: A for launch; revisit B if research shows the need.

State what you don't know, and what you'll measure to decide whether to revisit.

§3Handle Pushback

  • "We don't have time." — Show the cost of fixing later versus building it in now, and propose the smallest high-impact step.
  • "Our users don't use screen readers." — Accessibility also covers keyboard use, zoom, captions, and temporary injuries; and you may not see these users in analytics.
  • "It works in my browser." — Show the validator error, the audit finding, or a recorded session in another environment.
  • "Can't we do it later?" — Agree on a date, an owner, and a measurable exit criterion, so "later" is a plan, not a hope.

Follow up in writing: decisions, owners, and dates. A short summary after the meeting prevents the conversation from being forgotten.

Lead with the decision they need to make Start with the recommendation and the ask, then give the reasons. A one-page summary with options beats a technical deep dive for most executives.

§4Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Explaining in jargon ("ARIA roles and the accessibility tree")Listeners disengageDescribe the user and business outcome first, with technical detail as backup
Presenting only your preferred optionLooks like advocacy, not analysisShow alternatives and trade-offs
Using fear or exaggerated statisticsLoses credibilityUse accurate, sourced, locally relevant evidence
Practice
  1. Write a one-page proposal for a skeptical product manager that argues for fixing form labels and focus order on the checkout flow, with options and a recommendation.
  2. Practice a two-minute explanation of why <button> beats <div onclick> for a non-technical audience.
Key takeaways
  • Translate technical choices into outcomes: reach, risk, cost, and speed.
  • Offer options with costs and risks, plus a clear recommendation.
  • Handle objections with evidence, and close with owners and dates.
