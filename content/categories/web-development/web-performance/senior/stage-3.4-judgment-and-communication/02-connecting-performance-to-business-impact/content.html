Connecting Performance to Business Impact

Leaders fund what they believe pays back. The strongest case for performance work is evidence from your own users that speed changes outcomes they care about.

§1From Correlation to Evidence

Observational data is a start. You can show that visits with faster loads convert better. But a skeptical executive will ask the obvious question: maybe fast users are simply different, such as people on better devices who were going to buy anyway.

MethodStrengthWeakness
Correlation in analyticsCheap, uses existing dataConfounded: device, country, and intent differ
Matched or segmented comparisonControls for some confoundersStill observational
Before/after on a launchEasy to explainOther things changed too, such as seasonality and campaigns
A/B testRandomization isolates the effectNeeds traffic, tooling, and care
Deliberate slowdown testMeasures the cost of slowness directlyYou are harming some users, so keep it small and brief

Use the strongest method you can afford, and be honest about the limits of weaker ones. Credibility is worth more than a bigger number.

§2Running a Performance Experiment

  1. Pick an outcome that matters: conversion, sign-ups, revenue per visit, bounce, or pages per session.
  2. Define the change and the metric it should move, such as 'deferred scripts reduce p75 LCP by 400 ms'.
  3. Randomize at the user level, so a user always sees the same variant, and split traffic.
  4. Check that the variants really differ in speed. Measure the performance metric in both groups, or you may be testing nothing.
  5. Run long enough to cover weekly cycles and reach adequate sample size, and decide the stopping rule in advance.
  6. Analyze the outcome with proper statistics, including confidence intervals, not just whether it went up.
  7. Look at segments, since slow-device users often benefit most.
// Report the result with its uncertainty
Variant B (faster):  p75 LCP 2.3 s vs 3.1 s   (-0.8 s, verified)
Conversion:          3.42% vs 3.31%        (+3.3% relative, 95% CI +0.6% to +6.0%)
Sample:              410,000 visits per arm, 14 days
Mobile-low-end:      +7.9% relative

§3Translating Into Value

  • Convert to money or goals the audience uses: revenue, retained users, support tickets avoided, or infrastructure savings.
  • State assumptions and ranges. 'Likely worth $200k to $450k a year' is more credible than a single exact figure.
  • Use the right comparison. Compare against the cost of the work, and against other uses of the same team's time.
  • Include non-revenue benefits such as accessibility reach, brand trust, and lower bandwidth, but don't pad the case.
  • Use external studies as context only, and lead with your own numbers.

Also make the cost of inaction visible. A trend line showing vitals worsening and the share of users in the 'poor' band can be as persuasive as a promised gain.

Gotcha A faster variant that doesn't actually load faster for the test group proves nothing. Always verify the performance difference between arms before interpreting the business result.

§4Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Presenting correlation as proof of causationA skeptic dismantles the case and trust is lostUse an experiment, or state the limits openly
Not checking that the variants differ in speedYou may measure no real changeMeasure the performance metric in each arm
Quoting a single precise revenue figureFalse precision undermines credibilityGive ranges and state assumptions
Practice
  1. Design an A/B test for a performance change, with the outcome metric, split, duration, and a check that the variants differ in speed.
  2. Turn a hypothetical +2% conversion lift into a value range with stated assumptions.
Key takeaways
  • Correlation is a start, and randomized experiments isolate the effect of speed.
  • Verify the speed difference, run long enough, and report results with uncertainty.
  • Translate into goals the audience cares about, with ranges and honest assumptions.
