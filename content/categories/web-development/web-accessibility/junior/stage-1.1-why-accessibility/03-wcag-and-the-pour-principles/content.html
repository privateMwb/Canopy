WCAG & the POUR Principles

The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines turn "make it accessible" into testable requirements. This lesson covers how they are organized and the four principles behind every rule.

§1What WCAG Is

WCAG is published by the W3C's Web Accessibility Initiative. It is the most widely referenced technical standard for web accessibility and the basis for many laws. The major versions you will meet are 2.0 (2008), 2.1 (2018), and 2.2 (2023). Each newer version adds criteria without removing the older ones, apart from a few obsolete items.

Each requirement is a success criterion: a testable statement that a page either meets or does not. Every criterion has a conformance level.

LevelMeaningTypical use
AThe most basic barriers. Failing these blocks some users entirelyRequired for any claim of conformance
AARemoves the biggest and most common remaining barriersThe usual legal and procurement target
AAAHighest, and not achievable for all contentApplied selectively where it makes sense

When a contract or law says "WCAG 2.1 AA", it means every Level A and Level AA criterion in version 2.1.

§2The Four POUR Principles

All criteria sit under four principles. They are a useful mental checklist, because almost any barrier fits one of them.

PrincipleQuestion it asksExample failure
PerceivableCan users sense the content?An image with no text alternative, or light grey text on white
OperableCan users operate the interface?A menu that works only with a mouse hover
UnderstandableCan users understand the content and the interface?An error that says "Invalid input" without saying what is wrong
RobustDoes it work with current and future tools?A custom control with no role or name for a screen reader

§3From Principle to Test

WCAG nests four layers: principles, guidelines, success criteria, and techniques. Only the criteria are normative. Techniques show ways to meet them (or common failures), and they are advice rather than requirements.

Principle 1: Perceivable
  Guideline 1.1: Text Alternatives
    Success Criterion 1.1.1: Non-text Content (Level A)
      Technique: give the image an alt attribute describing its purpose
      Failure: image has no alt attribute

Criteria are numbered by principle, guideline, and criterion, so 1.4.3 means Principle 1, Guideline 4, Criterion 3 (Contrast Minimum). You will see these numbers in audit reports and automated tool output.

The key insight POUR is not a checklist to memorize. It is a way to classify any barrier you find. When you meet a problem with no obvious rule, ask which of the four principles it breaks.

§4Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Treating WCAG as a design review onlyMany criteria concern code, behavior, and content, which design review missesCheck markup, interaction, and content, not only visuals
Aiming for AAA everywhereWasted effort, and some AAA criteria cannot apply to all contentTarget AA, and add AAA criteria where they clearly help your users
Treating techniques as requirementsRigid rules, or false failures when another valid approach is usedJudge pages against the success criteria, using techniques as guidance
Practice
  1. Open a page and find one real barrier. Classify it under Perceivable, Operable, Understandable, or Robust, and note the matching success criterion number if you can.
  2. Look up Success Criterion 1.4.3 on the W3C "How to Meet WCAG" quick reference, and write the minimum contrast ratio for normal text in your own words.
Key takeaways
  • WCAG success criteria are testable, with levels A, AA, and AAA. AA is the usual target.
  • POUR (Perceivable, Operable, Understandable, Robust) groups every criterion into four ideas.
  • Criteria are normative. Techniques are helpful guidance, not extra requirements.
