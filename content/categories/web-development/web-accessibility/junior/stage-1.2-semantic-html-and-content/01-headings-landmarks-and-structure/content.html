Headings, Landmarks & Structure

Sighted users scan a page by its look. Screen reader users scan it by its structure. Headings and landmarks are the map that makes that possible, and you build them with plain HTML.

§1Headings Are an Outline

Headings give a page an outline. Screen reader users commonly list all headings and jump straight to the one they want, much as you would scan bold titles in a printed article. That only works if the headings reflect the real structure of the content.

  • Use one h1 that names the page or its main subject.
  • Nest levels in order: h2 sections contain h3 subsections. Do not skip from h2 to h4.
  • Choose a level for its meaning, not its size. Style the size with CSS.
  • Do not fake headings with bold text or a large div. Assistive technology will not see them.
<h1>Trail Running Guide</h1>
  <h2>Choosing Shoes</h2>
    <h3>Road-to-trail shoes</h3>
    <h3>Technical trail shoes</h3>
  <h2>Training Plans</h2>
    <h3>Beginner, 5 km</h3>

<!-- Bad: looks like a heading, but is invisible to assistive technology -->
<div class="big-bold">Training Plans</div>

§2Landmarks Mark the Regions

Landmarks label the major regions of a page. Screen readers let users jump between them, for example straight to the main content or the navigation. Native HTML5 elements create them automatically.

ElementLandmark roleUse for
header (direct child of body)bannerSite-wide header and logo
navnavigationA block of navigation links
mainmainThe unique content of the page (use one)
asidecomplementaryRelated content that stands apart
footer (direct child of body)contentinfoSite-wide footer
form or searchform or searchA form with an accessible name, or a search region

Inside an article or section, a header or footer is just a plain element, not a page-level landmark. If a page has more than one nav, give each a distinct label so users can tell them apart.

§3Putting It Together

A good page skeleton uses landmarks for regions and headings for the content inside them. Together they let a user move quickly at two levels: first to a region, then to a section within it.

<body>
  <header>
    <a href="/">Trailhead</a>
    <nav aria-label="Primary">...</nav>
  </header>

  <main>
    <h1>Trail Running Guide</h1>
    <section aria-labelledby="shoes-h">
      <h2 id="shoes-h">Choosing Shoes</h2>
      ...
    </section>
  </main>

  <aside aria-label="Related guides">...</aside>
  <footer>
    <nav aria-label="Footer">...</nav>
  </footer>
</body>

To check your work, use a heading and landmark view from a browser extension such as HeadingsMap or from your screen reader's elements list. If the outline reads sensibly with the page hidden, the structure is sound.

The key insight Structure is a feature for everyone. The same headings that help a screen reader user jump around also feed browser reading modes, search engines, and your own ability to maintain the page.

§4Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Choosing heading levels by font sizeThe outline becomes a confusing jumble of levelsPick levels by structure, and use CSS to control size
Building pages from only div elementsNo landmarks, so screen reader users cannot jump to main content or navigationUse header, nav, main, and footer
Several unlabeled nav elementsUsers hear "navigation" repeatedly and cannot tell them apartAdd distinct labels, such as aria-label="Primary" and aria-label="Footer"
Practice
  1. Pick a content-heavy page and list its headings in order. Does the outline make sense on its own? Fix any skipped or misused levels.
  2. View the same page's landmarks with a screen reader or extension. Is there exactly one main, and are repeated landmarks labeled?
Key takeaways
  • Headings form an outline. Use one h1, nest levels in order, and pick them for meaning rather than size.
  • Native elements (header, nav, main, aside, footer) create landmarks automatically.
  • Label repeated landmarks so users can tell them apart.
