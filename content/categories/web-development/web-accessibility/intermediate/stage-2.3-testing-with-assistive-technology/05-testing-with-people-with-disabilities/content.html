Testing with People with Disabilities

Every other kind of testing guesses at the experience. Watching someone who relies on assistive technology use your product shows you what actually happens, and it is almost always a surprise.

§1Why It Matters

Automated tools and your own screen reader practice find rule violations. They do not tell you whether people can accomplish their goals. Disabled users are the experts in their own tools. They navigate faster, use features you have never heard of, and hit problems you would not imagine.

This is also a matter of respect and accuracy. Do not assume that passing a checklist, or one person's feedback, speaks for everyone. People with the same condition work in very different ways.

§2Planning a Session

A usability session with disabled participants is like any other, with a few extra considerations.

StepGood practice
RecruitUse disability organizations, specialist recruiters, or your own users, and cover a range of needs and tools
CompensatePay participants fairly for their time and expertise, as you would any consultant
PrepareMake consent forms and materials accessible. Ask in advance about the setup and adjustments they need
Use their setupLet people use their own devices, settings, and assistive technology, whenever possible
Task-basedGive realistic goals ("book a flight"), not instructions on what to click
ObserveWatch, take notes, and ask what they are thinking. Do not rescue or lead
Session script (45 minutes)
1. Welcome, consent, how we'll record, and that we're testing the product, not you
2. Warm-up: "Tell me how you usually find and book things online."
3. Task 1: Find a flight from Chicago to Denver for next Friday.
4. Task 2: Change the seat selection after booking.
5. Wrap-up: "What was hardest? What would you change first?"
Notes: record where they hesitate, backtrack, or ask a question.

§3Turning Findings Into Action

The value comes from what you do with it. Group issues by the task they block, rank them by user impact, and share concrete observations, not just conclusions.

  • Record the barrier, what the person was trying to do, and what happened.
  • Share short clips (with permission) or quotes. They persuade teams more than a report.
  • Track the fix and retest with the same task, ideally with the same participants.
  • Respect privacy. Store recordings securely, and never identify participants without consent.

Make it a habit rather than a one-off. Regular sessions, plus a public feedback channel that people can use to report barriers, keep you close to real users. Prototype testing is also valuable, since it is far cheaper to fix a design than a shipped product.

The key insight One participant is one person's experience, not a verdict on a whole group. Use sessions to find real barriers and to understand them, and combine them with automated tests, expert review, and standards.

§4Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Treating one participant as representing a whole disability groupFixes serve one workflow and miss othersInclude several participants with varied needs and tools
Asking people to test for free, or only at the endDisrespectful, and problems are expensive to fix by thenPay participants, and test early with prototypes
Guiding people toward the "right" answerYou hide the very barriers you need to seeObserve quietly, and probe only after the task ends
Practice
  1. Write a three-task script for a product you work on, using realistic goals rather than click instructions.
  2. List five organizations or channels you could use to find participants who use assistive technology, and outline how you would compensate them.
Key takeaways
  • Testing with disabled people shows whether tasks can actually be completed, which tools cannot.
  • Pay participants, use their own setups, give realistic tasks, and observe without rescuing.
  • Turn findings into concrete, prioritized fixes, and retest after changes.
