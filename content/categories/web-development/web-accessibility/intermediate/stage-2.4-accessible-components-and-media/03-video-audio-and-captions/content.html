Video, Audio & Captions

Audio and video are inaccessible by default to people who cannot hear or see them. Captions, transcripts, and audio description are the three tools that fix that, and each serves more people than you might expect.

§1What WCAG Requires

WCAG's requirements for time-based media depend on whether the media is recorded or live, and whether it is audio, video, or both.

CriterionLevelApplies toRequirement
1.2.1AAudio-only or video-only (recorded)A transcript or equivalent alternative
1.2.2ARecorded video with audioCaptions
1.2.3ARecorded videoAudio description or a text alternative
1.2.4AALive video with audioLive captions
1.2.5AARecorded videoAudio description

Captions serve deaf and hard-of-hearing viewers, but also people in noisy places, people watching on mute, and language learners. Transcripts help search, skimming, and screen reader and braille users. Audio description narrates important visual information for blind and low-vision viewers, such as actions and on-screen text.

§2Good Captions

Captions are not just a transcript of speech. Good captions are:

  • Accurate. Automatic captions are a starting point and need human review, especially for names and technical terms.
  • Synchronized with the audio.
  • Complete. They include speaker identification where needed, and meaningful sounds such as [applause] or [door slams].
  • Readable. Short lines, sensible breaks, and enough time on screen.

§3Players, Autoplay, and Audio Description

The player itself must be accessible. Controls need names, be reachable by keyboard, and be usable by screen readers. Native video and audio controls are a good default. Custom players need the same care as any custom widget.

  • No autoplay with sound. If audio plays for more than three seconds, users need a way to pause or control its volume (WCAG 1.4.2).
  • Audio description: if the narration already describes what is shown, no extra description is needed. Otherwise, provide a described version, an extra audio track, or a text alternative with the visual details.
  • Transcripts: for podcasts and videos, a full transcript on the page, in HTML, is the simplest and most flexible alternative.
  • Live events: plan for live captioning, for example by a captioner or a service, before the event, not after.

Embedding third-party players (video platforms, social posts) hands part of the accessibility to someone else. Check that their controls are keyboard operable, and that captions are available and turned on where you can control it.

The key insight Captions are for sound, audio description is for visuals, and transcripts do both in text. Different people need different ones, and many benefit from all three.

§4Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Relying on unreviewed auto-captionsWrong words, missing speakers, and garbled terms mislead viewersCorrect captions by hand before publishing
Autoplaying video with soundDisrupts screen readers and startles usersDo not autoplay, or provide a clear way to stop audio
Video with important visuals and no descriptionBlind users miss information that is only shown on screenNarrate it, or add audio description or a text alternative
Practice
  1. Add captions and a transcript to a short video you have made. Check the caption file against the audio, correcting names and terms.
  2. Watch a video with the screen off, listening only. What information would you miss without audio description?
Key takeaways
  • Provide captions for audio in video, transcripts for audio-only content, and audio description for important visuals.
  • Review automatic captions, and make sure players are keyboard operable with named controls.
  • Avoid autoplay with sound, and plan live captions in advance.
