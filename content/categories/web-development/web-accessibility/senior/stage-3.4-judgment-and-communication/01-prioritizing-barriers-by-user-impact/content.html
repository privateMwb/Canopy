Prioritizing Barriers by User Impact

An audit of a real product can return hundreds of findings. Treating them as equal wastes effort and demoralizes teams. A senior engineer ranks barriers by what they do to real people, and can explain why.

§1Why WCAG Level Is Not Severity

WCAG levels describe the kind of requirement, not how much harm a failure does in a given product. A Level A failure on a decorative icon can be trivial, while a Level AA failure on the checkout button can stop every keyboard user from buying. Ranking by level alone sends teams to the wrong work.

Instead, judge each barrier by its impact on the task, who is affected, and how hard it is to work around.

SeverityMeaningExample
BlockerPrevents a user from completing a core task, with no workaroundKeyboard trap in the payment form
SeriousMakes a core task very hard, or forces a poor workaroundForm errors not announced to screen readers
ModerateCauses friction or confusion, but the task can be doneSkipped heading levels on an article
MinorA small annoyance or inconsistencyRedundant alt text on a decorative image

§2Factors That Decide Priority

Severity is the main factor, but it is not the only one. A fair prioritization weighs several.

  • Impact on the task: does it block, hinder, or merely annoy?
  • Reach: how many users and how many pages are affected? A bug in a shared component hits everything that uses it.
  • Journey criticality: sign-in, checkout, account recovery, and anything people cannot avoid rank higher than a rarely visited page.
  • Workaround: can someone get it done another way, such as a phone number or a different page?
  • Legal and contractual exposure: public-sector, regulated, or contractually committed areas carry extra weight.
  • Effort: cheap fixes with real benefit go first, and a high-effort low-impact fix may wait.

§3A Simple Scoring Model

A light scoring model keeps triage consistent, and makes the reasoning visible to people who disagree. Keep it simple, and adjust by judgment.

Priority score = Impact (1-4)  x  Reach (1-3)  x  Journey (1-3)  - Workaround (0-2)

Impact:     4 blocker, 3 serious, 2 moderate, 1 minor
Reach:      3 shared component or most pages, 2 several pages, 1 single page
Journey:    3 core (sign-in, checkout), 2 important, 1 peripheral
Workaround: 2 easy workaround exists, 1 awkward, 0 none

Example A: Keyboard trap in checkout modal
  4 x 2 x 3 - 0 = 24  -> fix now

Example B: Missing alt on a decorative banner on the About page
  1 x 1 x 1 - 2 = -1 -> fix in routine cleanup

The number is not the point. The point is that the team agrees on the factors, and that each finding comes with a short, clear statement of who cannot do what. "A screen reader user cannot complete payment" persuades far better than "violation of 4.1.2".

Re-rank as you learn. User reports, analytics on page traffic, and results from testing with assistive technology users will all change your view of what matters most.

The key insight Prioritization is how you turn an overwhelming audit into a plan people will actually follow. Rank by what stops people from doing what they came to do, and explain it in those terms.

§4Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Sorting findings by WCAG level onlyTrivial Level A items outrank serious Level AA blockersRank by task impact, reach, and journey criticality
Treating all findings as equalTeams lose heart, and effort goes into low-value fixesUse a consistent severity scale and scoring model
Reporting violations without describing user impactLeaders and developers do not see why it mattersSay who cannot do what, on which journey
Practice
  1. Take ten audit findings from a product you know, and rate each by impact, reach, journey, and workaround. Which three come first, and why?
  2. Rewrite three findings as "who cannot do what" statements suited to a product manager.
Key takeaways
  • WCAG level is not severity. Judge barriers by their impact on tasks.
  • Weigh impact, reach, journey criticality, workarounds, legal exposure, and effort.
  • Use a simple, shared scoring model, and always explain findings in terms of the people affected.
