Legal Landscape & Risk

Accessibility law is a patchwork that is changing quickly. A senior engineer does not need to be a lawyer, but does need to know where the obligations come from, how they are enforced, and how to reduce risk in practice.

§1A Map of the Main Obligations

This is a simplified overview, not legal advice. Dates and rules change, so check current guidance, and consult counsel for decisions.

§2Reading Risk Realistically

Risk is not equal across products. Think about three factors:

  • Who you serve: public bodies, funded institutions, and consumer-facing companies in regulated sectors face the clearest obligations.
  • Where you operate: a product sold in the EU, the US, and Canada must satisfy several regimes at once. WCAG 2.1 or 2.2 AA satisfies most of them.
  • Visibility and friction: high-traffic consumer pages, checkout flows, and sign-in are common litigation targets, because the barriers are easy to demonstrate.

Most legal exposure comes from basic failures on core journeys: unlabeled fields, inaccessible checkout, missing alt text, keyboard traps, and unreadable contrast. Those are exactly the issues that automated tools and the quick manual checks in this course find.

§3What Actually Reduces Risk

Risk falls when you can show a credible, continuing effort, and rises when you cannot.

PracticeWhy it helps
Adopt a target standardGives engineers and lawyers the same definition of "done" (for example, WCAG 2.2 AA)
Test core journeys regularlyFinds and fixes the barriers most likely to be challenged
Publish an accessibility statement and a feedback channelShows good faith and gives users a way to be heard before escalating
Keep a remediation plan with owners and datesDemonstrates progress on known gaps
Include accessibility in procurementReduces inherited third-party risk
Keep recordsEvidence of testing, decisions, and fixes

Be skeptical of quick fixes. Accessibility overlays, which are scripts that claim to fix a site automatically, have been criticized by disabled users and accessibility professionals, and regulators have taken action against some vendors' marketing claims. An overlay is not a substitute for fixing the underlying code, and it does not provide legal protection.

Not legal advice Treat this as a map for engineers. When a real obligation or a demand letter arrives, a lawyer who specializes in accessibility is the right person to interpret it. Your part is to have the evidence and the fixes ready.

§4Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Treating legal risk as a single global numberMisjudges obligations by region, sector, and productMap each product to the regimes that apply to it
Buying an overlay as a legal shieldDoes not fix underlying barriers, and invites criticism and riskFix the code, and test with real assistive technology
Ignoring a complaintTurns a fixable issue into a disputeRespond quickly, fix the barrier, and keep records
Practice
  1. For a product you work on, list every jurisdiction and sector rule that could apply, and the standard each one points to.
  2. List your product's five most legally visible journeys (for example sign-in, checkout), and check when each was last tested for accessibility.
Key takeaways
  • Most regimes point to WCAG 2.1 or 2.2 at Level AA, so that is the practical target.
  • Exposure concentrates on core journeys, so test sign-in, checkout, and forms first.
  • Credible, continuing effort (a standard, testing, statements, plans, records) reduces risk. Overlays do not.
