Accessibility Statements & Feedback Channels

An accessibility statement tells users, honestly, how accessible your site is and what to do when it is not. A feedback channel turns users into your early warning system.

§1What a Statement Is For

An accessibility statement is a public page that describes the accessibility of a site or app. In the EU and UK, public sector bodies are required to publish one, and the European Accessibility Act expects similar information from many businesses. Even where it is not required, a statement is good practice. It helps users decide whether and how to use your service, shows good faith, and gives people a path when something fails.

Link it from every page, typically in the footer, so it can be found without searching.

§2What Goes in It

A good statement is short, honest, and written in plain language. The EU's model statement shows the typical contents:

  • Commitment: a brief statement of what you aim to meet (for example, WCAG 2.2 AA).
  • Compliance status: fully, partially, or not compliant, and the scope the status covers.
  • Known issues: non-accessible content, with reasons (disproportionate burden, outside scope, and so on) and, where possible, workarounds and dates.
  • Preparation: when the statement was prepared, last reviewed, and how it was assessed (self-assessment or independent).
  • Feedback: how to report a problem or request content in another format, with contact details.
  • Enforcement: how to escalate if the response is not satisfactory, for example a regulator link.

The W3C publishes an accessibility statement generator that helps you assemble one.

§3Running a Feedback Channel

A statement with a dead email address is worse than none. Treat feedback as a product process with an owner.

  1. Offer more than one route: email, a form, and a phone number or similar. Make sure the form itself is accessible.
  2. Commit to a response time, and meet it. Say what users can expect.
  3. Triage like any bug: reproduce it, rate it by user impact, assign it, and track it in your backlog.
  4. Close the loop: tell the reporter what happened, and offer a workaround while a fix is pending.
  5. Learn from patterns: repeated reports in one area point to a systemic issue, not a one-off.

Also make requests for alternative formats routine: a transcript, large print, a plain-text version, or help completing a task. People should not have to justify the request.

The key insight Users who report a barrier are doing you a favor. A clear statement, an easy way to reach you, and a fast response turn a frustrating experience into a chance to fix the product and keep the relationship.

§4Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Claiming the site is "fully accessible"Almost never true, and discredited by the first user to find a barrierState the real status, and list known issues
Listing a contact that is never readReports vanish, and users lose trustAssign an owner, set a response time, and track every report
A statement that is never updatedOut of date within monthsReview it at every major release, and update the date
Practice
  1. Draft an accessibility statement for a site you know, including a known issue with a workaround and a date.
  2. Design a feedback process for accessibility reports: who receives them, how fast they reply, how issues are tracked, and how reporters are updated.
Key takeaways
  • Publish an honest statement covering status, known issues, preparation, feedback, and escalation, and link it from every page.
  • Offer more than one accessible way to give feedback, and respond within a stated time.
  • Treat reports as tracked work, close the loop with the reporter, and keep the statement current.
