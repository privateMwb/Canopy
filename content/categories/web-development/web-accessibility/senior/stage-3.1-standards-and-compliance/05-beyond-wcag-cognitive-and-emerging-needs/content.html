Beyond WCAG: Cognitive & Emerging Needs

WCAG is a floor, and it is strongest on needs that can be measured with a rule. Cognitive and learning disabilities, mental health, and new kinds of interfaces need design judgment that goes beyond the checklist.

§1The Cognitive Gap

WCAG 2.x covers cognitive and learning disabilities only partly, because many needs do not reduce to a single testable rule. Dyslexia, ADHD, autism, memory differences, anxiety, and acquired brain injury shape how people read, focus, remember, and decide. The W3C's COGA (Cognitive and Learning Disabilities Accessibility) work describes these needs and proposes design patterns that go beyond the formal criteria.

NeedPractices that help
Understand contentPlain language, short sentences, concrete examples, defined terms and abbreviations
Stay focusedFewer distractions, no auto-playing motion, one primary action per screen
RememberDo not require memorizing across steps, show progress, save work, allow review
PredictConsistent layout and navigation, labels that match behavior, no surprises
Recover from errorsForgiving input, clear messages, undo, and time to decide
Read comfortablyLeft-aligned text, generous spacing, no walls of text, avoid fully justified paragraphs

§2Designing for Cognitive Load

Cognitive accessibility is mostly about lowering the effort required to use a product. Many WCAG 2.2 additions (Redundant Entry, Accessible Authentication, Consistent Help) are examples. You can go further by treating effort as something to design down.

  • Break long tasks into clear steps, with a visible progress indicator.
  • Let users save and return later, and never wipe progress on an error.
  • Use familiar patterns and standard controls. Novelty costs attention.
  • Say what will happen before a user commits, and confirm after.
  • Avoid pressure: no countdown timers, "only 2 left" nagging, or tricks that rush a decision.

§3Emerging Interfaces

New interaction models arrive faster than standards. The core questions stay the same: can people perceive it, operate it, understand it, and trust it with the tools they use?

AreaAccessibility questions to ask
Voice and conversational interfacesIs there a text alternative, for people who cannot speak or hear? Are responses clear and interruptible? Can the chat window be operated by keyboard and screen reader?
AI-generated contentIs generated alt text, summary, or caption reviewed for accuracy? Is the interface accessible, and are errors recoverable?
AR, VR, and spatial interfacesAre there alternatives to motion and gesture, adjustable comfort settings, and captions for spatial audio? See the W3C XR Accessibility User Requirements.
PersonalizationCan users adjust text, simplify layouts, and choose symbols or modes? Standards such as WAI-Adapt aim to make this machine-readable.

WCAG 3.0 is being developed as a successor, with a different scoring model and broader scope. At the time of writing it is still a working draft, so WCAG 2.2 remains the standard to build and test against. Design with the underlying user needs, and the specific rules will follow.

The key insight Conformance and usability are different goals. Meeting every criterion tells you the page clears the floor. Asking whether a tired, anxious, or distracted person could finish the task tells you whether it is actually good.

§4Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Assuming WCAG conformance covers cognitive needsPages pass while remaining confusing or exhaustingAdd plain language, predictability, and low-effort flows, and test them with users
Pressure tactics such as timers and countdownsForce rushed decisions and exclude users who need timeRemove artificial urgency, and give people time
Shipping AI-generated alt text or captions uncheckedWrong or misleading text reaches users who depend on itReview generated text, and keep a human in the loop
Practice
  1. Take a multi-step flow and list where it asks users to remember, wait, or decide under pressure. Redesign two of those points to lower the effort.
  2. Pick one emerging interface (a chatbot, an AR app, an AI feature) and write five accessibility questions you would want answered before launch.
Key takeaways
  • WCAG covers cognitive disabilities only partly. Design for clarity, predictability, low memory load, and error tolerance.
  • Reduce effort: break tasks into steps, save progress, avoid pressure, and use familiar patterns.
  • For new interfaces, ask the same four questions: perceive, operate, understand, and trust. Build on WCAG 2.2 until a successor is final.
