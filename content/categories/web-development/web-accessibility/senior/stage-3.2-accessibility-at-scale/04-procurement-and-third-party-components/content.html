Procurement & Third-Party Components

Much of what users experience on your site was built by someone else: chat widgets, payment forms, cookie banners, video players, and component libraries. When they fail, your users still blame you, and so might a regulator.

§1Third-Party Risk Is Your Risk

A third-party component that inserts itself into your page becomes part of your page. If it traps focus, has no labels, or fails contrast, your site fails, even though you did not write it. Under many laws, responsibility stays with the organization that publishes the service.

So treat accessibility as a requirement when you choose what to buy or embed, and as an ongoing check afterward.

Typical third-party elementsCommon accessibility risks
Chat and support widgetsFocus traps, unlabeled controls, no keyboard access, no live announcements
Payment and checkout formsInaccessible iframes, errors not announced, inaccessible CAPTCHAs
Cookie and consent bannersFocus not managed, content hidden behind the banner, poor contrast
Video and media playersControls not keyboard operable, missing captions
Maps, calendars, and date pickersNo keyboard alternative, poor labeling

§2Evaluating a Vendor

Do not rely on a vendor's marketing. Collect evidence, and test it yourself.

  1. Ask for an ACR or VPAT, and read the remarks critically. Check the date, scope, and standard.
  2. Ask about their process: how they test, who tests, how they fix defects, and who owns accessibility.
  3. Run a proof of concept with your own checks: keyboard, screen reader, zoom, and automated tests, on your key tasks.
  4. Check for a track record: public accessibility statement, roadmap, and how they handled past reports.
  5. Score it. A simple rubric keeps decisions consistent and defensible.
Vendor evaluation (score 0-3 each)
Accessibility documentation (ACR, statement, date)    [ ]

§3Contracts and Ongoing Management

Put accessibility into the contract, not just the conversation:

  • Name the standard and level (for example, WCAG 2.2 AA, and EN 301 549 where relevant).
  • Require remediation timelines for defects, and notice of changes that could affect accessibility.
  • Give yourself the right to test and to receive updated reports.
  • Agree on remedies if the product does not conform, including termination for repeated failure.

After purchase, keep an inventory of third-party components and who owns each. Re-test after major updates, because a new vendor release can introduce new barriers. For open-source libraries, look at the issue tracker for accessibility reports, check whether widgets follow the Authoring Practices Guide, and read the documentation for keyboard behavior before you adopt.

Contract clause (example wording, adapt with counsel)
"The Supplier shall ensure that the Service conforms to WCAG 2.2 Level AA
and shall provide an Accessibility Conformance Report on request. The
Supplier shall remediate reported non-conformances within 30 days, and shall
notify the Customer before changes that may affect accessibility."
The key insight The cheapest moment to demand accessibility is before you sign. After a contract is in place, your leverage is mostly gone, and you are left with workarounds and complaints.

§4Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Accepting a vendor's claim of compliance without testingInaccessible products enter your product, and you own the problemRun a proof of concept with keyboard and screen reader tests
Embedding third-party scripts with no reviewBarriers appear without warning after vendor updatesKeep an inventory, assign owners, and retest after changes
A contract with no accessibility termsNo leverage to get defects fixedName the standard, timelines, reporting, and remedies
Practice
  1. List every third-party component on your most important page. For each, record who owns it and whether you have checked it with a keyboard.
  2. Use the vendor scoring sheet above on a product you are considering, or one you already use.
Key takeaways
  • Third-party components become part of your product, so their accessibility is your responsibility.
  • Evaluate with an ACR plus your own tests, and score vendors consistently.
  • Put the standard, remediation timelines, and testing rights in contracts, and re-test after updates.
