Migrations & Long-Term Maintenance

Upgrading without freezing development.

§1Software Ages

A full-stack app sits on top of a fast-moving stack: the framework, React, Node, the bundler, the ORM, the platform, and hundreds of packages. Left alone, the gap widens until upgrading becomes a project nobody wants to start, and security fixes land only in versions you can't adopt. Maintenance is a continuous activity, not a rescue operation.

§2A Sustainable Upgrade Practice

  • Small, frequent upgrades beat rare, huge ones. Patch and minor versions weekly or monthly through automated PRs (Renovate, Dependabot) with CI as the safety net.
  • Track support windows: know when your Node and framework major versions go out of security support and plan around them.
  • Read the changelog and migration guide first, and note breaking changes that touch your code.
  • Use codemods the framework provides to automate mechanical changes, then review the diff.
  • Upgrade one thing at a time: separate framework, runtime, and library upgrades so you can bisect problems.
  • Verify with real tests: the E2E and integration suites from Stage 2.4 are what make upgrades safe.

§3Handling a Major Migration

Large migrations, such as moving from the Pages Router to the App Router or from one framework to another, can't happen in a weekend. Treat them as incremental projects.

  1. Define the goal and the reason, so the business can weigh the cost.
  2. Migrate incrementally using coexistence (many frameworks let old and new routers run side by side) and the strangler pattern: route by route, new code replaces old.
  3. Pick the order: begin with low-risk, low-traffic pages to learn, and leave critical flows until the process is proven.
  4. Keep shipping features on a stable base; avoid a long-lived migration branch that diverges.
  5. Measure progress (percentage of routes migrated) and results (performance, error rates), and define when you'll stop.
  6. Decommission old code deliberately. Half-finished migrations leave two systems to maintain.

§4Reducing the Cost of Future Change

  • Keep business logic separate from framework code, so framework upgrades touch thin layers.
  • Prefer standard APIs (Web fetch, SQL, OpenTelemetry) over proprietary ones where practical.
  • Limit dependencies, and wrap the important ones behind your own interfaces.
  • Document decisions, so future maintainers know why, and when it's safe to change (lesson 3.4.4).
  • Budget recurring time for maintenance, such as a day each sprint, rather than waiting for a crisis.
Upgrade debt compounds Skipping two major versions doesn't cost twice as much; it often costs far more, because breaking changes interact and the community's answers stop applying to your version. Regular small upgrades are the cheapest insurance.

§5Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Ignoring upgrades until a security deadline forces themA rushed, high-risk, multi-version jumpUpgrade continuously in small steps
A long-running rewrite branchIt diverges from main, merges painfully, and delays valueMigrate incrementally in main using coexistence
Upgrading everything at onceYou can't tell which change caused a failureChange one major dependency at a time
Practice
  1. Check an existing project for outdated packages and end-of-life runtimes, and make a prioritised upgrade plan.
  2. Write an incremental migration plan for moving ten routes to a new router or framework, including the order, the success metrics, and the stopping point.
Key takeaways
  • Maintain continuously with small, automated, well-tested upgrades.
  • Migrate large changes incrementally with coexistence and the strangler pattern, and finish decommissioning.
  • Keep business logic and dependencies loosely coupled so future change stays cheap.
