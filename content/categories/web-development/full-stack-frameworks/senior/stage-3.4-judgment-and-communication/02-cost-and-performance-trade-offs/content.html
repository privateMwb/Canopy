Cost & Performance Trade-offs

Serverless economics and scaling limits.

§1Where the Money and the Limits Are

Serverless and managed platforms change how you pay: you're billed for usage rather than for machines. That is a bargain for spiky or low traffic and can become expensive at scale. Pricing models and limits change frequently, so always check current published numbers before relying on a comparison.

§2Serverless Economics

A rough model helps. Suppose a route is invoked 20 million times a month and averages 200 ms at 1 GB of memory: that is about 4 million GB-seconds of compute. Compare that figure at current prices with the cost of a small always-on server or container that could handle the same load. Below some traffic level serverless is cheaper (you pay nothing while idle). Above it, steady high traffic often favours reserved capacity, because you're paying for idle waiting (for slow upstream calls, for example) at a premium.

// Back-of-envelope: replace the numbers with real, current prices
const invocations = 20_000_000;
const seconds = 0.2, gb = 1;
const gbSeconds = invocations * seconds * gb;          // 4,000,000 GB-s
const monthly = gbSeconds * PRICE_PER_GB_SECOND + invocations * PRICE_PER_INVOCATION;
// Compare with: container instance(s) + ops time + headroom for spikes

Include the human cost. A server you operate costs engineering time, on-call, patching, and scaling work. A managed bill that's twice as high may still be cheaper than half an engineer.

§3Limits That Shape Architecture

  • Execution time and memory per function: long jobs need queues, workflows, or dedicated workers.
  • Request and response size: large uploads should go direct to storage (lesson 2.2.4).
  • Concurrency and connection limits: many instances can exhaust a database; use pooling.
  • Cold starts and regional placement: influence latency (lesson 3.1.4).
  • Platform lock-in: features that exist only on one platform raise the cost of leaving.

§4Performance as a Cost Lever

Speed and cost usually improve together. A cached response costs almost nothing to serve, while a dynamic one costs compute, database load, and often bandwidth. Moving work from request time to build time, pushing more through the CDN, and trimming payloads reduce both latency and bills. Set budgets and alerts on spend, and watch for runaway usage, such as an unbounded loop of function invocations or a bot scraping an expensive route.

Do the arithmetic early Teams are often surprised by their first big bill because nobody modelled it. A twenty-minute estimate with current pricing and your expected traffic, repeated each quarter, beats discovering the answer on an invoice.

§5Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Comparing platforms only by headline priceBandwidth, add-ons, and engineering time dominate in practiceModel total cost of ownership, including operations
No spend alerts or limitsAn abuse spike or bug creates a surprise billSet budgets, alerts, and rate limits on expensive routes
Assuming serverless is always cheaper (or always pricier)The wrong platform for your traffic shapeModel your own usage against current prices
Practice
  1. Estimate monthly cost for an app with 5M requests, 200 ms average duration, and 500 GB of transfer on two different hosting models using current prices.
  2. Find the three most expensive routes or assets in an app, and propose one change for each that reduces cost without hurting users.
Key takeaways
  • Costs come from compute, bandwidth, data, builds, and observability, so model each against current prices.
  • Serverless suits spiky or low traffic; steady high load may favour reserved capacity, and operations time is a real cost.
  • Caching and lighter pages reduce both latency and spend; set budgets and alerts to catch surprises.
