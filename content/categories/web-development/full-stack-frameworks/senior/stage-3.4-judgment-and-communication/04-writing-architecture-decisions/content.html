Writing Architecture Decisions

Design docs and RFCs for full-stack systems.

§1Why Write Decisions Down

Architecture is mostly a series of trade-offs made with incomplete information. If the reasoning lives only in someone's head, future engineers either repeat old debates or undo good choices for forgotten reasons. An Architecture Decision Record (ADR) is a short document capturing one significant decision: the context, the options, the choice, and the consequences.

§2A Practical ADR Template

# ADR-014: Host the app on a managed platform (not self-hosted containers)

Status:    Accepted (2026-03-10)    # Proposed | Accepted | Superseded by ADR-0xx
Deciders:  Platform lead, Engineering manager

## Context
What forces are at play? Requirements, constraints, team size, deadlines, risks.

## Options considered
1. Managed platform: fast setup, usage-based cost, some vendor coupling
2. Self-hosted containers: more control and cost predictability, more operations work
3. ...

## Decision
We will use option 1, because ...

## Consequences
Positive: ...   Negative: ...   Risks and mitigations: ...
Revisit when: monthly bill exceeds $X, or we need a region the platform lacks.

Keep it to one or two pages. Store ADRs in the repository, next to the code, numbered and never deleted. A reversed decision gets a new ADR that supersedes the old one.

§3Writing for a Skeptical Reader

The most important reader is the person who disagrees. Write so that they can see their objections were understood and answered.

  • State the problem before the solution, and make sure the reader agrees it's a problem.
  • Present real alternatives fairly, including the strongest argument for each. A straw-man option list destroys trust.
  • Be explicit about trade-offs: what you're giving up, not only what you're gaining.
  • Use evidence: measurements, prototypes, cost estimates, and incident history, rather than appeals to authority or fashion.
  • Name the risks and the tripwires: the conditions under which the decision should be revisited.
  • Separate facts, assumptions, and opinions, and label each.

§4From Document to Decision

Circulate a draft early (an RFC), invite review from the people affected, and set a deadline for comments. Disagreement is useful input, so record it, even if the decision goes the other way. 'Disagree and commit' works only when people feel heard. Finally, communicate the outcome beyond the review group, and link the ADR from the code and the runbooks it affects.

The consequences section is the honest part Anyone can list reasons for the option they like. A strong ADR spells out what gets harder, what it will cost, and when the team should reconsider. That's what lets a skeptic trust the rest.

§5Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Writing the ADR after the decision, as justificationIt reads as advocacy and hides the real alternativesDraft it while deciding, and use it to think
Listing only options you'd already dismissedReviewers see through the straw men and distrust the conclusionInclude credible alternatives with their best arguments
No revisit conditionsA decision hardens into dogma after circumstances changeState the signals that should trigger a review
Practice
  1. Write an ADR (one page) for choosing a framework and hosting model for a hypothetical product, including at least two credible alternatives and revisit conditions.
  2. Have a colleague play a skeptical reviewer and list their objections. Revise the ADR to answer each one with evidence.
Key takeaways
  • ADRs capture context, options, decision, and consequences in a short, versioned document.
  • Write for the skeptic: fair alternatives, explicit trade-offs, and evidence rather than assertion.
  • Include revisit conditions, gather review early, and record the outcome where the code lives.
