Compliance & Privacy Basics

Handling personal data responsibly.

§1Personal Data Is a Liability

Any data that identifies a person (names, emails, IP addresses, device ids, locations, health or financial details) carries legal and ethical obligations. You can't outsource the responsibility to a framework. The safest data is the data you never collected.

§2Principles That Show Up in Code

PrincipleWhat it means for engineering
Data minimisationCollect only what you need; don't store what you can compute or discard
Purpose limitationUse data only for the purpose you stated
Retention limitsDelete data on a schedule; don't keep logs and backups forever
Access control and auditRestrict who can read personal data, and log access to sensitive records
Security of processingEncrypt in transit and at rest; protect backups; manage keys
User rightsSupport access, export, correction, and deletion requests

§3Engineering for Privacy Rights

  • Know where personal data lives: keep a data inventory covering databases, logs, analytics, backups, search indexes, and third-party processors.
  • Make deletion real: a delete request must reach caches, search indexes, analytics tools, and vendors, not just the main table. Design data models so it's possible (for example, a user-scoped key).
  • Export on request in a structured, machine-readable format.
  • Consent and cookies: don't load non-essential tracking until the user has consented, where the law requires it.
  • Scrub logs and error reports: don't send personal data to monitoring tools unnecessarily.
  • Segregate sensitive fields and encrypt them at the application level where the risk justifies it.

§4Regulations in Brief

Laws such as the EU's GDPR, California's CCPA/CPRA, and sector rules such as HIPAA (US health data) and PCI DSS (payment cards) impose obligations that depend on where your users are and what data you hold. Many apply based on your users' location, not yours. The usual practical shortcuts, such as using a payment provider so card numbers never touch your servers, dramatically reduce your scope. This lesson is an engineering orientation, not legal advice: involve counsel for real compliance decisions.

§5When Things Go Wrong

Have an incident response plan that includes breach notification. Some laws require notifying regulators within tight deadlines (for example, 72 hours under GDPR for qualifying breaches), so decide ahead of time who investigates, who decides, and who communicates.

Delete by design, not by heroics Deletion requests are easy when each person's data is keyed and discoverable, and a nightmare when it's scattered across logs, backups, and vendor tools. Build the inventory and the deletion path before the first request arrives.

§6Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Logging full request bodies containing personal dataSensitive data spreads into logs and vendors with long retentionLog identifiers and redact sensitive fields
Treating 'delete account' as deleting one table rowData persists in analytics, search, backups, and processorsMaintain a data inventory and automate deletion across it
Collecting data 'just in case'More breach impact and more compliance obligationCollect only what a defined purpose requires
Practice
  1. Make a data inventory for an app you know: every place a user's email address is stored or sent, including logs and vendors.
  2. Design a 'delete my account' flow that reaches every location on that list, and decide what must be retained for legal reasons.
Key takeaways
  • Collect less, keep it for less time, and restrict access to what remains.
  • Plan for user rights (export, correction, deletion) across every system that holds personal data.
  • Compliance depends on users and data types, so involve legal expertise and have an incident response plan.
