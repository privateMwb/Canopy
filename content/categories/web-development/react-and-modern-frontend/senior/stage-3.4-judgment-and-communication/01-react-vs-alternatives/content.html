React vs. Alternatives

Choosing a front-end technology is a judgment about problems, people, and time. An honest comparison names what each option is genuinely good at, and what it costs, without cheerleading.

§1The Landscape, Honestly

Every option below is used successfully in production. The differences are real but smaller than online arguments suggest, and they matter most at the edges of team size, performance needs, and project lifespan.

§2What Actually Drives the Decision

Raw benchmark speed rarely decides real projects. The factors that do are mostly about people and constraints.

  • Team skills and hiring: the best framework is often the one your team already knows well; React's ecosystem makes hiring and finding libraries easier, but that advantage matters only if you will hire and use those libraries.
  • Application shape: a content site, an interactive dashboard, an offline-capable tool, and a collaborative editor have different needs. A content-heavy site may need very little client JavaScript at all.
  • Team size and structure: large teams benefit from strong conventions (Angular's, or a disciplined React architecture); small teams benefit from less ceremony.
  • Performance envelope: for low-end devices or strict budgets, compile-time and fine-grained approaches or islands reduce JavaScript; React with Server Components and the Compiler narrows the gap.
  • Ecosystem needs: component libraries, data tooling, testing, accessibility primitives, and mobile (React Native) reuse.
  • Longevity and risk: project age, the framework's governance and stability, upgrade history, and how hard migration would be.
  • Meta-frameworks matter as much as the library: Next.js, Remix/React Router, Nuxt, SvelteKit, Astro, and Angular's tooling decide routing, rendering, and data loading in practice.

§3Making and Defending the Call

A senior engineer separates evidence from preference and fashion. Compare options against *your* requirements, and be honest about unknowns.

  1. Write the requirements and constraints first: performance budgets, SEO, team, timeline, integrations, lifespan.
  2. Shortlist two or three options and prototype the riskiest feature in each, rather than comparing marketing pages.
  3. Score against the requirements, including soft factors (hiring, learning curve, migration cost), and note where you are uncertain.
  4. Consider reversibility: how expensive is it to change later? Prefer choices that keep business logic framework-independent (plain TypeScript modules, a clean API layer).
  5. Decide and record the reasoning, alternatives rejected, and the conditions that would make you reconsider.
Weak argumentStronger version
"React is the industry standard""We can hire for it and our key libraries (X, Y) exist for it, which matters because..."
"Svelte is faster""Our target devices are low-end; a prototype shows 60% less JS for our main screen"
"Frameworks are bloat""Our app is a mostly static content site; client JS adds cost with no benefit"
"We have always used it""Migration would cost N engineer-months and our problems are not caused by the framework"

Beware two failure modes: resume-driven development (picking the new thing for its own sake) and inertia (refusing to look at better fits). Most important of all, remember that architecture, discipline, and testing usually matter more to outcomes than the library label.

Compare on your requirements, not on reputation A fair comparison starts from your constraints and a prototype of your riskiest feature. Most disagreements dissolve when you ask what problem each option solves for this team, and what it costs.

Tying business logic to the frameworkMigration becomes a rewriteKeep domain logic in plain, framework-independent modules

Practice
  1. Pick a project type (content site, dashboard, collaborative editor) and write which two options you would shortlist and the riskiest feature to prototype in each.
  2. Rewrite three weak arguments for or against a framework as evidence-based statements.
  3. List which parts of an app you know are framework-specific and which could survive a migration unchanged.
Key takeaways
  • Each option is genuinely good at something; the real differences are team, ecosystem, application shape, and performance envelope.
  • Evaluate by prototyping the hardest feature against written requirements, not by reputation.
  • Keep domain logic portable, record your reasoning, and state when you would revisit the decision.
