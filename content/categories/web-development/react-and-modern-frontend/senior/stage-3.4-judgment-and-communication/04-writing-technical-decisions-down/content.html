Writing Technical Decisions Down

Front-end decisions (a state library, a rendering strategy, a design system) outlive the meeting where they were made. A short, structured record of the choice and its reasoning is how a team remembers why, and notices when the reasons stop being true.

§1Why Write Decisions Down

Six months later nobody remembers why the app uses one state library, why that route is server rendered, or why a promising approach was rejected. Without a record, debates repeat, newcomers undo reasonable choices because the reasons are invisible, and no one notices when the assumptions behind a decision change. Writing also improves the decision, because vague thinking is hard to hide on a page.

FormatScopeLengthAnswers
ADR (Architecture Decision Record)One decisionHalf a page to a page"What did we decide, and why?"
RFC / design docA feature, system, or migration, written before building2 to 6 pages"What should we build, how, what are the alternatives, and what are the risks?"

Keep both in the repository (a docs/adr/ folder) or a shared wiki where people will actually find them, and treat them as part of the engineering record. Number ADRs, never delete them, and mark old ones superseded by new ones, so the history of reasoning stays intact.

§2The Shape of a Good Record

# ADR 012: Server state moves to TanStack Query; Redux only for the cart

Status: Accepted (2026-10-12)    Supersedes: ADR 004
Deciders: Mina (frontend lead), Jorge (platform), Dee (product eng)

## Context
~70% of our Redux code caches API responses by hand (loading flags, invalidation, refetch).
Stale data bugs: 11 tickets in Q3. Every new endpoint adds ~120 lines of slice/thunk code.

## Decision
Fetch and cache server data with TanStack Query. Keep Redux Toolkit only for client state
that is shared and changes often (cart draft, editor selection).

## Options considered
1. Keep Redux for everything: no migration cost, but the stale-data bugs and boilerplate continue.
2. RTK Query: stays in the Redux ecosystem; less familiar to the team and ties caching to the store.
3. TanStack Query (chosen): built-in caching, dedup, retries; works with our router and Suspense plan.

## Consequences
+ Less code per endpoint; fewer stale-data bugs; simpler tests with MSW.
- Two state tools to learn; migration of 34 slices over ~3 months, alongside feature work.
- Query keys need conventions (see key factory in /docs/queries.md).

## Revisit if
Offline-first or complex cross-entity normalization becomes a requirement, or the library's
maintenance status changes.
  • Context: the situation, constraints, and the problem with numbers (tickets, load times, costs).
  • Decision: stated plainly, near the top, in one or two sentences.
  • Options considered: the real alternatives, including "do nothing", each described fairly.
  • Consequences: benefits and costs you are knowingly accepting. A record with no downsides is not credible.
  • Revisit conditions: what would make you reconsider, so the decision has a built-in expiry trigger.
  • Status and ownership: proposed, accepted, superseded; who decided; when.

§3Design Docs for Migrations and Writing for Skeptics

A design doc for a larger change, such as moving a client-rendered app to server rendering, adds sections that make the risk visible: goals and non-goals, proposed design (with a diagram), rollout plan, risks and mitigations, rollback plan, success metrics, and open questions.

SectionWhat it must contain
Goals / non-goalsMeasurable outcomes (LCP p75 from 4.1 s to under 2.5 s); what you are deliberately not doing
DesignThe approach, boundaries, data flow; a diagram; how it fits the current architecture
RolloutPhases, feature flags, who is affected, how you monitor each step
Risks you acceptWhat could go wrong, likelihood, impact, and the mitigation or why you accept it
RollbackHow to return to the previous state at each step, and how long it takes
MetricsHow you will know it worked: Web Vitals, error rate, build time, cost
Open questionsWhat you do not know yet, and how you will find out
  • Lead with the conclusion and the ask, so busy reviewers know what you want in the first paragraph.
  • Frame the problem with evidence: measurements and user impact beat preference or authority.
  • Present alternatives at their strongest. A straw-man rejects your credibility along with the option.
  • Be honest about uncertainty, and say how a spike or benchmark would resolve it.
  • Name risks and a rollback plan. Reviewers trust proposals that say how they could fail.
  • Invite disagreement early: circulate a draft, ask specific questions, and record objections and how they were resolved.
  • Keep it short, scannable, and edited: headings, small tables, one diagram, and nothing that does not support the decision.
  • When circumstances change, write a new record that supersedes the old one rather than editing history.
Record the reasoning, not just the result "We use X" is visible in the code. "We chose X over Y and Z because of these constraints, accepting these costs, and we would revisit if..." is the knowledge that disappears, and the part worth writing down.

§4Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Recording only the decision, not alternatives or reasonsFuture readers cannot tell if it still appliesInclude context, options, consequences, and revisit conditions
Straw-manning the rejected optionsSkeptical reviewers lose trustDescribe each alternative at its strongest
Leaving out the risks and rollback plan in a migration docReviewers cannot judge safety; approvals are shakyAdd explicit risks, mitigations, and a tested rollback
Editing an old ADR when things changeThe reasoning history is lostWrite a new ADR that supersedes the old one
Practice
  1. Write a one-page ADR for a front-end decision you made recently, including options considered, consequences, and revisit conditions.
  2. Draft a design doc outline for migrating a client-rendered app to a server-rendered architecture, with goals, rollout phases, risks you accept, and a rollback plan.
  3. Ask a colleague to read your draft cold and explain in their own words what was decided and why; revise until they can.
Key takeaways
  • ADRs capture one decision and its reasoning; design docs plan larger changes with rollout, risks, and rollback.
  • A strong record states the decision first, treats alternatives fairly, and is honest about costs and unknowns.
  • Write for the skeptical reader, add revisit conditions, and supersede records rather than rewriting them.
