Build vs. Buy

Evaluating services, frameworks, and platforms.

§1A Decision With Long Tails

Should we build our own authentication, queueing, search, or billing, or adopt a managed service, an open-source framework, or a vendor product? The choice shapes your costs, speed, risk, and team focus for years. Teams err in both directions: building what they should have bought (and maintaining it forever), or buying what is core to their advantage (and surrendering it).

§2Start With Strategic Value

Ask first: is this a differentiator for our business? Capabilities that customers choose you for deserve your best engineers and your control. Commodity capabilities (sending email, processing card payments, hosting, authentication for most products) rarely distinguish you, and others do them better and cheaper at scale.

QuestionLeans toward buildLeans toward buy
Is it core to our competitive advantage?YesNo, it is a commodity
Do we have unusual requirements that products do not meet?Yes, and they are essentialNo, standard needs
Do we have the expertise to build and operate it well?Yes, and capacityNo, or it would divert from core work
Is time to market critical?NoYes
Is it security- or compliance-sensitive in ways that specialists handle better?NoYes (payments, identity, regulated data)

§3Count the Whole Cost

Building looks cheap because the first version is visible and the rest is hidden. Buying looks cheap because the license fee is visible and the rest is hidden. Compare total cost of ownership over several years:

  • Building: initial development, then maintenance, upgrades, security patches, on-call, scaling, documentation, onboarding new engineers, and the opportunity cost of what those engineers would have built instead. Software that is built is never finished.
  • Buying: license or usage fees (and how they scale with growth), integration and migration effort, customization limits, training, vendor management, and the cost of eventually leaving.
  • Open source is not free: it saves license fees but you still pay for operating, upgrading, patching, and understanding it, and projects can lose maintainers or change their licenses.

§4Evaluate Vendors and Frameworks Like a Risk Manager

  • Lock-in: how hard is it to leave? Can you export all your data in usable formats? Does the integration spread vendor-specific concepts through your whole codebase? Put a thin adapter (the ports-and-adapters idea from stage 3.1) around the vendor.
  • Reliability and security: their uptime history, SLAs and what compensation means in practice, certifications, how they handle incidents, and what data they hold.
  • Viability: financial health, roadmap, community size, release cadence, and what happens if they are acquired or shut down.
  • Fit and limits: rate limits, quotas, latency, regional availability, data residency, and customization ceilings. Prototype against your hardest requirement first.
  • Pricing dynamics: how the bill grows with usage, price-change history, and where the cliffs are. A tool that costs $200 a month today may cost $200,000 at 100x scale.

§5Hybrid and Reversible Choices

It is rarely all or nothing. You can buy first and build later once you understand the problem and have scale that justifies it, which is often the best path to a good in-house version. You can build the core and buy the edges. And you can make decisions reversible with interfaces, data export, and incremental migration plans. Reversibility lowers the stakes: choose faster when the choice is cheap to undo, and slower when it is not.

Beware the not-invented-here and shiny-object traps Engineers are drawn to building (it is interesting) and to adopting the newest tool (it is exciting). Neither is a business reason. Ask who will operate it at 3 a.m. in two years, and whether the work moves the product forward more than the alternative would.

Practice
  1. Choose a capability in your system (search, email, feature flags, auth) and write a one-page ADR comparing build, buy, and a hybrid, with a revisit condition.
  2. Take a vendor you rely on and answer: how would we leave, how long would it take, and what would we lose?
  3. Estimate the three-year total cost of building a feature in-house, including maintenance and on-call, and compare it with the vendor price at 10x usage.
Key takeaways
  • Build what differentiates you; buy commodities, unless your needs are unusual and you can operate it well.
  • Compare total cost of ownership, including maintenance, operations, scaling prices, and exit costs.
  • Reduce lock-in with adapters and data export, prefer reversible choices, and record decisions in an ADR.
