Incident Response & Postmortems

Debugging live systems and learning from outages.

§1When Production Breaks

Every system eventually has an incident: an unplanned event that harms users. How well you respond depends less on individual heroics than on a calm, practiced process. The aims, in order: stop the harm, restore service, then understand why, so it does not recur.

§2Roles and Structure

RoleResponsibility
Incident commander (IC)Coordinates. Decides priorities, assigns tasks, keeps the group focused. Does not dig into debugging.
Operations / respondersInvestigate and apply mitigations.
Communications leadUpdates stakeholders, support, and the status page on a regular cadence.
ScribeKeeps a timestamped log of what was observed, hypothesized, and done.

In a small team one person may wear several hats, but separating coordinating from fixing matters: it keeps someone looking at the whole picture while others look at a corner of it. Use a single shared channel or call, and declare severity early (for example SEV1 = major user-facing outage), because severity sets who is notified and how fast.

§3Mitigate First, Diagnose Second

Resist the urge to find the root cause while users are suffering. The fastest route to recovery is usually a mitigation that does not require understanding: roll back the last deploy, flip a feature flag off, fail over, scale up, shed load, or block a bad client. Root-cause analysis can wait until the bleeding stops.

# A calm first five minutes
1. Acknowledge the page. Open the incident channel. State: "I'm IC. Severity SEV2 until we know more."
2. Assess impact: which users, which journeys, since when? (SLO dashboard, error rates, support tickets)
3. Ask: WHAT CHANGED? Recent deploys, config or flag changes, migrations, traffic spikes, dependency incidents.
4. If a recent change correlates: roll it back FIRST, investigate afterwards.
5. Post a first status update within ~10 minutes, then at a fixed cadence (e.g. every 20-30 min), even if "no change".

§4A Method for Diagnosis

  • Form hypotheses and test them. State one ("the DB connection pool is exhausted"), name the evidence that would confirm or refute it, and check it. Avoid trying changes at random.
  • Narrow the scope using breakdowns: which endpoint, tenant, region, version, or dependency is affected? Partial failures point to the cause.
  • Follow the request path using traces and logs, from the edge inward, to find where latency or errors begin.
  • Change one thing at a time and write down when you did, or you will not know what worked.
  • Escalate early. Bring in the owner of the failing dependency sooner rather than later; calling for help is a strength.
# Useful questions, and where to look
Is it all traffic or a slice?     -> metrics grouped by route / tenant / region / build version
Did latency or errors start suddenly? -> sharp step = change or dependency; slow ramp = leak or growth
Is a dependency slow or down?     -> dependency dashboards, circuit-breaker state, trace spans
Are we saturated?                 -> CPU, memory, event-loop lag, DB connections, queue depth, disk
Is it new code?                   -> deploy markers, canary vs stable comparison

§5Blameless Postmortems

After resolution, write a postmortem (also called a retrospective or incident review). The goal is learning, not punishment. People make reasonable decisions with the information they have; when an engineer's action caused an outage, the real question is why the system made that mistake easy and the dangerous action possible without safeguards. A culture that blames individuals hides problems, and hidden problems recur.

# Postmortem: Checkout errors on 14 March

Summary      One-paragraph description: what broke, impact, duration.
Impact       Users affected, failed orders, revenue, SLO budget consumed.
Timeline     Timestamps (UTC): first symptom, detection, escalation, mitigation, resolution.
Root causes  Contributing factors, usually several (technical AND process).
What went well / What went badly / Where we got lucky
Detection    How did we find out? Could we have known sooner?
Action items Specific, owned, dated: each tied to a cause and prioritized.
Lessons      What this teaches about the system.
  • Ask "why" repeatedly, but stop at systems, not people. Rather than "an engineer ran the wrong command," ask why the tool allowed it, why there was no confirmation or staging check, and why it took 20 minutes to notice.
  • Seek multiple contributing factors. Serious outages rarely have a single "root cause"; they are alignments of several weaknesses.
  • Make action items real: owned, scheduled, and tracked to completion. A postmortem whose actions are never done is theater.
  • Share them widely. Other teams learn from your incidents, and publishing builds trust with customers (a public summary for major incidents).
Practice before you need it Run game days: simulate an outage, such as killing a database replica or failing a dependency, and let the on-call practice. Test the pager, the runbooks, and the communication path when the stakes are zero. Teams that rehearse recover in minutes; teams that improvise recover in hours.

§6Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Hunting for root cause while users are still affectedOutages last far longer than neededMitigate first (roll back, flag off, fail over), then investigate
Everyone debugging, nobody coordinatingDuplicated effort, conflicting changes, silent stakeholdersName an incident commander and a communications lead
Blaming a person in the postmortemPeople hide mistakes, and the system's weaknesses stayExamine how the system allowed the error; fix the system
Practice
  1. Write the first-five-minutes checklist for your own service, including the three mitigations you would try first.
  2. Take a past outage (yours or a public postmortem from a company) and rewrite its timeline and contributing factors in blameless form.
  3. Run a 30-minute game day: break something in staging without telling the on-call engineer, and have them practice the full response.
Key takeaways
  • In an incident, stop the harm first: mitigate with rollback, flags, or failover before searching for root cause.
  • Use clear roles (incident commander, communications, scribe) and hypothesis-driven diagnosis, changing one thing at a time.
  • Hold blameless postmortems that find systemic causes and produce owned, tracked action items.
