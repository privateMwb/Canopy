Building an Accessibility Program

Individual heroes cannot keep a large product accessible. A program turns accessibility from one person's passion into an organizational habit, with owners, standards, training, and support from the top.

§1Why a Program, Not a Project

A project has an end date. Accessibility does not. Products change weekly, teams turn over, and new features arrive faster than any audit. Without a program, the same issues return in each release, and the one person who understands them becomes a bottleneck.

A program makes accessibility someone's job and everyone's responsibility. It has an executive sponsor, a clear standard, named owners, training, tooling, and ways to measure progress. The W3C's Accessibility Maturity Model describes four rough stages organizations pass through: *inactive*, *launch*, *integrate*, and *optimize*.

§2Roles and Ownership

A common and effective structure pairs a small central team with distributed champions, so expertise is shared and no one is overloaded.

RoleResponsibility
Executive sponsorSets the mandate, secures budget, and removes blockers
Accessibility lead or teamOwns the standard, tooling, audits, training, and escalations
Champions in each teamSpot issues early, answer first questions, and connect to the central team
Designers and content authorsBuild accessibility into design and copy before development
Engineers and QAImplement, test, and verify against the standard
Procurement and legalEvaluate vendors and manage compliance and risk

Make ownership explicit in each product team, for example with an accessibility owner named in the team's working agreement. Without a name, responsibility spreads thin and disappears.

§3A Practical Roadmap

Start small and visible, then widen. A typical first year might look like this:

Days 0-30: Foundations
  - Get an executive sponsor and a one-page policy
  - Choose the target standard (e.g. WCAG 2.2 AA)
  - Baseline audit of the 3-5 most important user journeys

Days 30-90: Quick wins and enablement
  - Fix the highest-impact blockers on those journeys
  - Add automated checks and a PR checklist
  - Run role-based training for design, engineering, and QA
  - Name champions in every product team

Days 90-180: Integrate
  - Add accessibility to the definition of done
  - Put accessibility requirements in procurement
  - Publish an accessibility statement and feedback channel

Days 180-365: Measure and improve
  - Track a small set of metrics, and review them quarterly
  - Audit the design system, and fix legacy hotspots in priority order
  - Plan next year based on what the data shows
  • Training should match roles: designers need color, layout, and annotations; engineers need semantics, keyboard, and ARIA; content authors need plain language and alt text.
  • Executive support is not decoration. It decides whether accessibility is allowed to delay a release.
  • Celebrate progress. Share wins, and show real user stories, to keep momentum.
The key insight The goal of a program is to make accessible work the default path for every team, not to make a central team do all the work. If the program needs you in every conversation, it is not yet scaled.

§4Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Relying on one accessibility specialistA single point of failure and an unscalable bottleneckBuild champions in every team, and train by role
Starting without executive supportAccessibility loses every trade-off against deadlinesSecure a sponsor and a written policy first
Trying to fix everything at onceOverwhelms teams and stalls progressPrioritize core journeys, and expand in phases
Practice
  1. Draw the ownership map for an organization you know: who owns the standard, who tests, who fixes, and who decides trade-offs? Mark the gaps.
  2. Draft a one-page accessibility policy that names the target standard, scope, roles, and escalation route.
Key takeaways
  • A program makes accessibility sustainable, through ownership, standards, training, and support from leaders.
  • Combine a small central team with champions in every product team, and train each role on what it needs.
  • Start with core journeys and quick wins, then integrate, measure, and expand.
