Pointer & Touch Targets

Not everyone can tap a tiny target precisely, and not everyone can perform a swipe or a drag. Bigger targets and simple alternatives to gestures make a page easier for far more people than you might expect.

§1How Big Is Big Enough

Small targets cause misses for people with tremor, limited dexterity, or large fingers, and for anyone using a phone on a moving bus. WCAG 2.2 added a minimum, and a more generous recommendation sits above it.

StandardSizeLevel
WCAG 2.5.8 Target Size (Minimum)At least 24 by 24 CSS pixels, with exceptions such as enough spacing around smaller targetsAA
WCAG 2.5.5 Target Size (Enhanced)At least 44 by 44 CSS pixelsAAA
Platform guidanceRoughly 44 points on iOS, and 48 density-independent pixels on AndroidRecommendation

In practice, aim for about 44 pixels for primary controls on touch devices, and never go below 24.

§2Making Targets Bigger

You can enlarge the clickable area without changing the visual design, using padding or a pseudo-element.

.icon-button {
  min-width: 44px;
  min-height: 44px;
  display: inline-flex;
  align-items: center;
  justify-content: center;
}

/* Enlarge the hit area of a small visual element */
.close {
  position: relative;
}
.close::after {
  content: "";
  position: absolute;
  /* adds 12px of tappable space on every side */
  inset: -12px;
}

Also leave space between targets. Two small buttons packed side by side make mistakes likely, and adjacent targets with enough spacing can satisfy the minimum even when each is small.

§3Gestures Need Alternatives

Several criteria protect people who cannot perform complex pointer actions:

  • Pointer Gestures (2.5.1, Level A): anything that needs multi-finger or path-based gestures, such as pinch or swipe, must also work with a single tap or click.
  • Dragging Movements (2.5.7, Level AA, new in 2.2): anything done by dragging needs a single-pointer alternative, such as buttons to move an item up or down.
  • Pointer Cancellation (2.5.2, Level A): do not fire an action on the down event. Use the up event (such as click) so users can slide away to cancel a mistaken press.
  • Label in Name (2.5.3, Level A): the accessible name must contain the visible label, so voice users can say what they see.
<!-- A sortable list: drag works for some, buttons work for everyone -->
<li>
  Task A
  <button type="button" aria-label="Move Task A up">Up</button>
  <button type="button" aria-label="Move Task A down">Down</button>
</li>
The key insight Large targets and simple alternatives are inclusive by design. They help people with tremor or injuries, but also anyone with a cracked screen, wet hands, or a bumpy commute.

§4Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Tiny icon buttons packed closely togetherFrequent mis-taps and frustrationUse at least 24 by 24 pixels (aim for 44) with space between targets
Features that need a swipe, pinch, or drag onlyExcludes people who cannot perform those motionsOffer a single-tap or button alternative
Triggering actions on pointer downNo chance to cancel a mistaken pressActivate on click (the up event)
Practice
  1. Inspect a mobile page in DevTools and measure three small controls. Which fall below 24 CSS pixels, and which fall short of 44?
  2. Take a drag-and-drop or swipe interaction from an app you know, and design a button-based alternative for it.
Key takeaways
  • Make targets at least 24 by 24 CSS pixels (WCAG 2.5.8), and aim for about 44 for primary touch controls.
  • Gestures like swiping, pinching, and dragging need a simple single-pointer alternative.
  • Fire actions on release, and keep accessible names matching visible labels.
