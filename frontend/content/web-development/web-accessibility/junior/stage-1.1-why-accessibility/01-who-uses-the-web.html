Who Uses the Web

Accessibility is not about a handful of edge cases. It is about the real range of people, devices, and situations your page meets every day, whether or not you ever see them.

§1Disability Is Diverse

When people hear "accessibility" they often picture one thing, usually blindness. In reality, barriers fall into several broad groups, and each group needs different support from your page. The World Health Organization estimates that around 1.3 billion people, roughly one in six, live with a significant disability.

AreaExamplesWhat helps
VisualBlindness, low vision, color blindnessText alternatives, sufficient contrast, zoom and reflow, screen reader support
AuditoryDeafness, hard of hearingCaptions, transcripts, visual alerts
MotorTremor, paralysis, limb loss, repetitive strainFull keyboard operation, large targets, no precision gestures required
CognitiveDyslexia, ADHD, memory and attention differencesPlain language, consistent layout, clear errors, no unnecessary time limits
Speech and neurologicalSpeech impairment, seizure disorders, vestibular disordersAlternatives to voice input, no flashing content, reduced motion options

Most of these groups are not "all or nothing". A person with low vision may use zoom on one task and a screen reader on another. Treat each need as a spectrum rather than a label.

§2Permanent, Temporary, and Situational

Disability is not only a permanent condition. The same barrier can affect someone for a lifetime, a few weeks, or a few minutes. This framing, often called the persona spectrum in inclusive design, shows how many people a single fix serves.

AbilityPermanentTemporarySituational
Touch / use of one armPerson with one armBroken arm in a castHolding a baby or a coffee
SeeingBlindnessEye surgery recoveryBright sun glare on a phone
HearingDeafnessEar infectionNoisy train or silent office
Speaking / readingDyslexia or a speech impairmentLaryngitisReading in a second language

Add age, slow networks, old devices, and tired or stressed users, and "edge case" stops being the right phrase. Anyone can be in any of these columns on any day.

§3One Fix, Many Beneficiaries

Features built for disabled users routinely help everyone. Captions serve deaf viewers, but also people in noisy places, people watching with the sound off, and people learning the language. Keyboard support serves people who cannot use a mouse, but also power users and anyone whose trackpad just died.

The takeaway is practical: you do not need to know who your users are, or whether they disclose anything, to build for them. You only need to remove barriers.

The curb-cut effect Sidewalk curb cuts were won by wheelchair users, yet they now serve parents with strollers, travelers with luggage, and delivery workers. Accessibility features follow the same pattern: designing for the margins tends to improve the whole product.

§4Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Thinking accessibility means "blind users"Motor, cognitive, hearing, and low-vision barriers get ignoredConsider the full range of needs listed above for every feature
"None of our users have disabilities"Most people never disclose, and analytics cannot detect assistive technology reliablyAssume disabled users are already there, and design accordingly
Designing only for an ideal setupThe page fails in glare, on one hand, on slow networks, and on small screensTest under constrained conditions, not just on a fast desktop
Practice
  1. Pick a page you use daily. List three situations (for example bright sunlight, one free hand, a loud room) where it would be hard to use, and what would fix each.
  2. For one feature you have built, write down who benefits from captions, keyboard support, and strong contrast beyond the group they were designed for.
Key takeaways
  • Barriers span vision, hearing, movement, cognition, and more, and each group needs different support.
  • Disability can be permanent, temporary, or situational, so anyone can be affected on any given day.
  • Fixes designed for the margins usually improve the experience for everyone.
