Assistive Technologies

Assistive technologies are the tools people use to reach your page. They do not read your pixels. They read what your code tells the browser, so the quality of your markup decides what they can do.

§1Screen Readers

A screen reader converts what is on screen into synthesized speech or braille. It does not read the visual page. It reads the browser's accessibility tree, a structured version of the page built from your HTML (roles, names, states, and relationships).

Screen readerPlatformNotes
NVDAWindowsFree and open source, widely used
JAWSWindowsCommercial, long established, widely used
VoiceOvermacOS and iOSBuilt in to Apple devices
TalkBackAndroidBuilt in to Android
NarratorWindowsBuilt in to Windows

Screen reader users rarely listen to a page top to bottom. They jump by headings, landmarks, links, and form controls, then read the part they care about. A page without structure forces them to listen to everything, like a book with no chapters.

§2Other Ways People Use the Web

Screen readers are only one tool. Many people use a combination of settings, hardware, and software, and your page needs to tolerate all of them.

TechnologyWho uses itWhat your page must do
Magnification and zoomPeople with low visionStay usable at 200% zoom and beyond without horizontal scrolling
High contrast / forced colorsPeople with low vision or light sensitivityKeep content and borders visible when the system overrides your colors
Voice controlPeople with motor impairments, or who cannot use their hands temporarilyGive controls visible text names so "click Save" works
Switch devicesPeople with severe motor impairmentsSupport keyboard-style operation with a logical order
Eye and head trackingPeople with limited movementProvide large, well spaced targets
Braille displaysPeople who are deafblind or prefer brailleProvide clean text and correct structure

Notice that most of these depend on the same foundations: real text, real controls, a sensible order, and visible names.

§3Why Markup Is the Interface

Assistive tools can only use what the browser exposes. Native HTML elements come with role, name, state, and keyboard behavior built in. A generic element has none of that until you add it.

<!-- Native: role=button, focusable, Enter and Space work, name comes from text -->
<button type="button">Save</button>

<!-- Generic: no role, not focusable, no keyboard support, no useful name -->
<div class="btn" onclick="save()">Save</div>

A voice control user can say "click Save" to the first and may not be able to reach the second at all. A screen reader announces "Save, button" for the first and just "Save" (if anything) for the second. Same pixels, very different experience.

The key insight Assistive technologies are consumers of your semantics. If your markup is correct, many tools work with no extra effort. If it is wrong, no amount of visual polish repairs it for the people who depend on it.

§4Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Testing only with a mouse and your eyesBarriers for keyboard, voice, and screen reader users stay invisibleInclude keyboard and screen reader checks in your routine
Using generic elements for controlsMissing roles, focus, and keyboard support, so tools cannot operate themUse button, a, input, and other native elements first
Assuming a screen reader reads the page top to bottomPages without headings and landmarks become slow and tiring to navigateProvide a clear heading and landmark structure
Practice
  1. Turn on a built-in screen reader (VoiceOver, TalkBack, or Narrator) and try to find the main content of a news site using only headings.
  2. Turn on your system's voice control, open a page, and try to activate three buttons by speaking their visible names. Note any that fail.
Key takeaways
  • Screen readers read the accessibility tree built from your markup, not the visual page.
  • People use many tools, including zoom, forced colors, voice, switches, and braille, and all depend on solid foundations.
  • Native HTML gives role, name, state, and keyboard behavior for free. Generic elements do not.
