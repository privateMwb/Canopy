Sectioning Elements

header, nav, main, article, section, aside, and footer name the regions of a page so browsers and assistive technology can navigate them.

§1The Page-Level Landmarks

A typical page has a banner, navigation, one main content area, and a footer. Each has a dedicated element, and most of them are exposed to screen readers as landmarks that users can jump between.

<body>
  <header>
    <a href="/">Canopy</a>
    <nav aria-label="Primary">
      <ul>
        <li><a href="/learn/">Learn</a></li>
        <li><a href="/about/">About</a></li>
      </ul>
    </nav>
  </header>

  <main>
    <h1>Welcome</h1>
  </main>

  <footer><p>&copy; 2026 Canopy</p></footer>
</body>

Use exactly one visible <main> per page for the content that is unique to it. <header> and <footer> directly inside <body> act as the site banner and site footer.

§2article, section, and aside

These three group content inside <main>, and the difference is whether the content stands on its own.

ElementUse it forTest
<article>A self-contained piece: blog post, comment, product cardWould it make sense syndicated on its own?
<section>A themed group within a page, normally with a headingDoes it have a natural heading?
<aside>Tangential content: sidebar, pull quote, related linksCould it be removed without hurting the main flow?
<main>
  <article>
    <header><h1>Pruning Tomatoes</h1></header>
    <section>
      <h2>When to prune</h2>
      <p>...</p>
    </section>
    <aside><p>Related: staking guide</p></aside>
  </article>
</main>

§3Headers and Footers Are Not Only Page-Level

Inside an <article> or <section>, a <header> holds introductory content such as a title and byline, and a <footer> holds closing material such as author or tags. Those nested ones are not landmarks; they simply describe their own section.

When in doubt, use a div <div> and <span> are fine when nothing semantic fits. A <section> without a heading is usually just a <div> with extra steps, and over-sectioning adds noise for screen reader users.

§4Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Wrapping the whole page in <section> elements by defaultDozens of unnamed regions with no meaningUse section only for themed groups that have a heading
Using more than one visible <main>Skip-to-content and landmark navigation become ambiguousKeep a single <main> per page
Putting site navigation inside <aside>It is announced as tangential content instead of navigationUse <nav> for major navigation blocks
Using <nav> for every group of linksLandmark lists become clutteredReserve it for primary, secondary, breadcrumb, and pagination navigation
Practice
  1. Mark up a blog home page with header, nav, main, three articles, an aside, and a footer.
  2. Turn on a screen reader or the browser's accessibility tree and list the landmarks it finds.
Key takeaways
  • Use header, nav, main, and footer for page structure, with one main.
  • article is self-contained, section is a headed group, and aside is tangential.
  • Don't use sectioning elements where a plain div would do.
