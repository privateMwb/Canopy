Writing Technical Decisions Down

Decisions that live only in a meeting or someone's head are lost within a year. A short, structured record of what you chose and why, written for a reader who was not there, is among the highest-leverage things a senior engineer produces.

§1Why Write Decisions Down

Six months later, nobody remembers why the team picked a state library, abandoned a framework, or put the cache where it is. Without a record, the same debates repeat, new hires reverse good choices because the reasons are invisible, and the team cannot tell when a decision's assumptions have changed. Writing also improves the decision itself: unclear thinking is hard to hide in a document.

There are two common formats, for two different scales of decision:

FormatScopeLengthQuestion it answers
ADR (Architecture Decision Record)One decisionHalf a page to a page"What did we decide, and why?"
Design doc (RFC)A feature or system, before building2–6 pages"What should we build, and how, and what are the alternatives?"

Both are written before or while the work happens, in the repository or a shared wiki where people will find them, and treated as part of the engineering record, not as paperwork.

§2Anatomy of an Effective Record

A strong decision record has a consistent shape. Readers should be able to grasp the decision in 30 seconds and the reasoning in five minutes.

# ADR 014: Use native <dialog> instead of a modal library

Status: Accepted (2026-10-12)   Supersedes: ADR 006
Deciders: Priya (frontend lead), Sam (accessibility), Lee (platform)

## Context
We ship three modal dialogs and depend on `react-modal` (24 KB) for focus trapping and Esc handling.
The library lags our React upgrades. All supported browsers (see support policy) ship `<dialog>`.

## Decision
We will use the native `<dialog>` element with `showModal()` and a thin wrapper component.

## Options considered
1. Keep `react-modal`: no work, but ongoing upgrade friction and 24 KB.
2. Adopt a headless UI library: more features than we need; another dependency.
3. Native `<dialog>` (chosen): focus trapping, Esc, backdrop, and `inert` background built in.

## Consequences
+ Smaller bundle, no dependency to upgrade, better default accessibility.
- We must polyfill for the 0.3% of sessions on older Safari, and write our own animation.
- Team must learn dialog focus-return behaviour.

## Revisit if
Support policy adds browsers without `<dialog>`, or we need nested modals with complex stacking.
  • Context: the situation, constraints, and the problem, in terms a newcomer can follow.
  • Decision: stated plainly, first, in one or two sentences.
  • Options considered: the real alternatives, including "do nothing", each with its genuine pros and cons.
  • Consequences: both the benefits and the costs you are accepting. A record with no downsides is not credible.
  • Revisit conditions: what would make you reconsider, so the decision has an expiry trigger.
  • Status and ownership: proposed, accepted, superseded, who decided, and when.

§3Writing for a Skeptical Reader

Write for the person who will challenge you, because that is who benefits most. A skeptical reviewer wants to know that you understood the problem, considered alternatives fairly, and are honest about the trade-offs. Several habits make the difference.

  • Lead with the conclusion. Busy readers should know the proposal and the ask in the first paragraph, then read on for evidence.
  • Frame the problem before the solution, with numbers: "INP is 380 ms at p75 on product pages" is stronger than "the page feels slow".
  • Present alternatives fairly, in their strongest form. Straw-man alternatives destroy credibility and the reviewer notices.
  • Be honest about what you do not know, and say how you would find out (a spike, a benchmark, a trial).
  • Name the risks and the rollback plan. Reviewers trust a proposal that says how it could fail and how you would recover.
  • Use concrete evidence: measurements, prototypes, links to data, and user impact, rather than preference or authority.
  • Keep it short, scannable, and edited. Use headings, small tables, and one diagram if it helps; cut anything that does not support the decision.
  • Invite disagreement early: circulate a draft, ask specific questions, and record objections and how they were resolved.

A decision is rarely made by the document alone, but the document makes the conversation faster and the outcome durable. When the context changes, write a new record that supersedes the old one rather than editing history, so the trail of reasoning stays intact.

Record the reasoning, not just the result "We use X" is a fact anyone can see in the code. "We chose X over Y and Z because of these constraints, and we would revisit if..." is the knowledge that disappears, and the part worth writing.

§4Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Recording only the decision, not the alternatives or reasonsFuture readers cannot judge whether it still holdsInclude context, options considered, and consequences
Straw-manning the alternativesSkeptical readers lose trust; the real trade-offs are hiddenDescribe each option at its strongest
Burying the conclusion at the endBusy reviewers never reach the pointState the decision and the ask first
Editing an old record when circumstances changeThe history of reasoning is lostWrite a new record that supersedes the old one
Practice
  1. Write a one-page ADR for a past technical decision you made, including options considered, consequences, and revisit conditions.
  2. Take a decision you disagree with and rewrite the case for it in its strongest form, then note what evidence would change your mind.
  3. Have a colleague read your draft cold and tell you, in their own words, what was decided and why; revise until they can.
Key takeaways
  • Written decision records preserve context and let teams revisit choices when assumptions change.
  • A strong record states the decision first, gives fair alternatives, and is honest about costs and unknowns.
  • Write for the skeptical reader with evidence, a rollback plan, and revisit conditions; supersede rather than rewrite.
