Choosing a Framework

Next.js, Remix, Nuxt, SvelteKit, and when plain beats all of them.

§1Start from Requirements, Not Hype

Framework choice is a decision under uncertainty, and the loudest advice online comes from people with a stake in a particular answer. Start by writing down what you actually need: rendering needs (static, dynamic, personalised), team skills, hosting constraints, ecosystem requirements, expected lifetime, and how much you're willing to bet on a vendor.

§2The Landscape

OptionStrengthsWatch out for
Next.jsLargest React ecosystem, mature hosting support, RSC and caching features, huge talent poolFast-moving concepts, caching complexity, features that work best on one vendor's platform
Remix / React Router 7Web-standards focus, simple data loading and mutations model, runs anywhereSmaller ecosystem, different mental model from RSC-heavy approaches
Nuxt (Vue)Excellent developer experience, strong conventions, flexible rendering per routeVue ecosystem is smaller than React's
SvelteKitSmall bundles, simple reactivity, great performanceFewer libraries and engineers to hire
AstroContent sites with minimal JavaScript, bring-your-own UI frameworkLess suited to highly interactive, app-like products
Plain React SPA + separate APIClear separation, simple hosting, no framework lock-inYou assemble SEO, routing, and data loading yourself; weaker first paint
Server-rendered monolith (Rails, Django, Laravel) + sprinkles of JSVery productive, batteries included, minimal frontend complexityRich client interactivity needs extra work

Capabilities and popularity shift every year, so treat this table as a starting point for research, not a verdict.

§3A Decision Process

  1. List the hard requirements: SEO, personalisation, offline use, compliance, hosting limits.
  2. Weight the soft factors: team experience, hiring market, ecosystem, performance, time to first release.
  3. Prototype the riskiest part (not a todo app) in the top two candidates, for a day or two.
  4. Assess exit cost: how hard is it to leave? Framework-specific APIs sprinkled everywhere increase it.
  5. Decide, write it down, and set a review point instead of relitigating it constantly.

Sometimes the right answer is the boring one: the framework your team already knows well, or no framework at all. A marketing site with a handful of pages may need static HTML, not a full-stack runtime.

Optimise for reversibility You can't predict the next five years of the ecosystem. Keep business logic in plain modules, isolate framework-specific code at the edges, and choose tools whose removal wouldn't mean a rewrite.

§4Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Choosing by popularity or novelty aloneA poor fit for your actual requirements and teamEvaluate against written requirements and a prototype
Prototyping with a trivial demoThe real risks (auth, data, caching, scale) go untestedPrototype the hardest feature in each candidate
Ignoring operational and hosting implicationsFeatures work only on one platform, and costs surprise you laterCheck the hosting story and exit cost up front
Practice
  1. Write the requirements for a real or imagined product, score three frameworks against them, and justify the winner in a paragraph.
  2. Identify which parts of an existing app are framework-specific, and estimate the cost of migrating them to another framework.
Key takeaways
  • Choose from written requirements and team realities, not hype.
  • Prototype the riskiest feature, and weigh the exit cost, hosting story, and ecosystem.
  • Keep business logic framework-agnostic so the decision stays reversible, and consider the boring option.
