Designing APIs for Other Teams

Treating consumers as customers.

§1Your Consumers Are Your Customers

An internal API used by three other teams is a product, and those teams are its customers. They did not choose your design, they cannot easily switch away, and every awkward decision you make is multiplied across everyone who integrates. The senior skill here is less about clever endpoints than about empathy, clarity, and stability: making it easy to do the right thing, hard to do the wrong thing, and safe to depend on you.

§2Start From the Consumer's Job

  • Interview consumers before designing. Ask what they are trying to accomplish, what they do today, and what hurts. Design around those jobs, not around your database tables.
  • Write the integration first. Draft the sample client code (or an OpenAPI spec plus an example call sequence) and review it with a real consumer before building anything. If the happy path takes eight calls, the design is wrong.
  • Expose domain concepts, not internals. An API that mirrors your schema leaks every future refactor to your consumers. Model what the business means (POST /orders/{id}/cancellation), not how you store it.
  • Make the common case trivial and the advanced case possible: sensible defaults, optional parameters, and no mandatory knowledge of your internals.
# A design-review checklist for an API other teams will use
Naming       Consistent, domain-language names; one word per concept across the API.
Shape        Same pagination, filtering, error format, and timestamp format on every endpoint.
Errors       Machine-readable code + human message + how to fix it; stable codes consumers can switch on.
Idempotency  Safe retries on every operation with side effects (Idempotency-Key).
Evolution    How will this change without breaking callers? What is the deprecation policy?
Limits       Documented rate limits, payload limits, and timeouts; clear 429 behavior.
Auth         Least-privilege scopes; how do consumers get credentials and rotate them?
Docs         Reference + a quickstart + worked examples; a sandbox consumers can safely try.
Observability Request ids in responses; consumers can quote them when reporting problems.

§3The Contract Is a Promise

Once consumers depend on behavior, all observable behavior is the contract, including things you never intended to promise: field order, undocumented fields, error messages, and response times. (This is sometimes called Hyrum's Law.) Reduce accidental promises by documenting what is guaranteed, returning only what is specified, and being explicit about what is not guaranteed.

  • Publish a stability policy: what counts as a breaking change, how much notice you give (for example 90 days), and how deprecation is signaled.
  • Be additive by default, as in stage 2.1, and version only when you must.
  • Announce changes where consumers will see them: a changelog, a mailing list or channel, and headers like Deprecation and Sunset on affected responses.
  • Track who uses what (per-client usage by endpoint and version), so you can contact the right teams before removing anything.

§4Reduce Integration Cost

  • Great documentation is part of the API. A reference generated from an OpenAPI spec, a five-minute quickstart, copy-pasteable examples, and an explanation of concepts and common workflows.
  • Provide a sandbox and test data so teams can integrate without touching production or waiting on you.
  • Offer SDKs or generated clients where many consumers share a language; keep them thin and generated from the spec.
  • Make errors teach. "invalid_request" is useless; "currency must be an ISO 4217 code such as USD" lets the developer fix it without asking you.
  • Give consumers a support path with an owner and a response expectation. An API with no one to ask is a liability.

§5Build Feedback Loops

Treat the API like any product: watch adoption and errors by consumer, review integration pain points regularly, and keep a roadmap that consumers can see. A short office hours slot or a shared channel often surfaces misunderstandings long before they become incidents. When a consumer needs something you will not build, say so clearly, explain the trade-off, and suggest an alternative; a fast, honest "no" is better than months of silence.

Say no to protect the interface Every field, endpoint, and option you add is a permanent commitment. A mature API owner declines requests that serve one consumer at the cost of everyone's clarity, and offers extension points (webhooks, generic filters) rather than special cases.

§6Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Mirroring your database schema in the APIEvery internal refactor becomes a breaking change for consumersModel domain concepts and keep storage details private
Changing behavior with no notice or usage dataConsumers break in production and lose trustPublish a stability policy, deprecate with notice, and track per-client usage
Shipping without docs, examples, or a sandboxEvery consumer interrupts you with the same questionsTreat documentation and test environments as part of the deliverable
Practice
  1. Pick an internal API in your system and write the quickstart a new consumer would need. Where do you struggle to explain it?
  2. Review one existing endpoint against the checklist above and list the three gaps with the highest consumer impact.
  3. Draft a stability and deprecation policy (what is breaking, notice period, how it is announced) in half a page.
Key takeaways
  • Treat internal APIs as products: start from consumers' jobs, prototype integrations, and expose domain concepts.
  • All observable behavior becomes a promise, so document guarantees, be additive, and deprecate with notice and usage data.
  • Lower integration cost with great docs, sandboxes, helpful errors, and a clear support path.
