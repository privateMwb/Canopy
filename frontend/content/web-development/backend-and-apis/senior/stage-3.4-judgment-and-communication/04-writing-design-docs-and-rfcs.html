Writing Design Docs & RFCs

Getting buy-in for technical change.

§1Writing Is How Engineers Scale

A design that lives only in your head can be reviewed by no one. A design document (or RFC, Request for Comments) is a written proposal for a significant technical change, shared before building so that colleagues can catch problems, improve the approach, and agree on direction. Writing also forces you to think: gaps in your reasoning that you could ignore while coding become obvious on the page. For senior engineers, producing clear documents that move a group to a decision is a core part of the job.

§2When to Write One

  • The change is hard to reverse (data models, public APIs, service boundaries, choice of core technology).
  • It affects several teams or has cross-cutting operational, security, or cost consequences.
  • Reasonable people could disagree on the approach.
  • It is a large effort where being wrong is expensive.
  • Not for every change: a small, easily reversible tweak does not need a document. Match the weight of the process to the risk.

§3Anatomy of a Strong Design Doc

# RFC-042: Move report generation to an async job system

Status: Proposed | Author: A. Rivera | Reviewers: Data, Platform, SRE | Decision by: 2026-03-14

## 1. Summary (3-5 sentences, readable on its own)
Reports currently run inside web requests and time out above 10k rows. We propose generating them
in background workers and delivering them via a download link. Delivers in ~6 weeks, 2 engineers.

## 2. Context and problem
Evidence, not adjectives: 4.2% of report requests fail (timeouts); support tickets; SLO impact.

## 3. Goals and non-goals
Goals:     Reports up to 1M rows; no timeouts; p95 request latency unaffected.
Non-goals: Real-time/streaming reports; redesigning report contents.

## 4. Proposal
Architecture diagram, data model, API (POST /reports -> 202; GET /reports/{id}), failure handling.

## 5. Alternatives considered
A) Increase timeouts          - rejected: ties up web workers, hurts all users.
B) Precompute nightly         - rejected: stale data; poor fit for ad-hoc filters.
C) Third-party reporting tool - viable; see build-vs-buy appendix; rejected on cost at 10x volume.

## 6. Risks, trade-offs, and open questions
Eventual completion adds UX complexity; queue backlog under load; storage cost of generated files.

## 7. Rollout and migration plan
Flag per tenant; shadow-run on internal tenant; 1% -> 25% -> 100%; rollback = flag off.

## 8. Operations and security
Metrics, alerts, SLOs, capacity estimate, access control on download links, data retention.

## 9. Success metrics
Timeout rate < 0.1%; p95 time-to-report < 2 min; no increase in web p95.
  • Lead with a summary that a busy VP or a new hire can read in thirty seconds and understand the ask.
  • State the problem with evidence before the solution. Reviewers cannot judge a solution to a problem they do not accept.
  • Be explicit about non-goals. They prevent scope creep and endless debates over things you are deliberately not doing.
  • Take alternatives seriously. Include the options you rejected and why, fairly. If you cannot articulate why the alternatives are worse, you are not ready. This is the section skeptical reviewers read most closely.
  • Be honest about risks and unknowns. Naming weaknesses builds credibility; hiding them gets them discovered in review or in production.
  • Include a rollout and rollback plan, the operational story (monitoring, on-call), and how you will know it worked.

§4Writing for a Skeptical Reviewer

Imagine your toughest reviewer: knowledgeable, busy, and unconvinced. Anticipate the questions they will ask and answer them in the document: What happens when this fails? Why not the simpler option? How does it scale to 10x? What does it cost? Who maintains it? What are we giving up? Use concrete numbers (capacity estimates, latency budgets, cost projections), diagrams for structure, and tables for comparisons. Replace claims ("this will be faster") with measurements or explicit estimates and their assumptions.

§5Running the Review

  • Circulate early, while the design can still change, and tell reviewers what kind of feedback you need ("I am least sure about the failure handling in section 4").
  • Invite the right people: owners of affected systems, security, operations, and a skeptic. Early objections from stakeholders are cheaper than late ones.
  • Use comments for specifics and a synchronous meeting for contention. Long threads of disagreement resolve faster in conversation; then record the outcome in the document.
  • Separate concerns from preferences. Address blocking issues (correctness, safety, cost) with changes; acknowledge style preferences without letting them stall the proposal.
  • Set a decision date and a decider. Consensus is nice but not required; someone must be accountable for choosing, so that review ends in a decision rather than drift.
  • Record the outcome (approved, changes required, rejected) and keep the doc as a record of why, which is invaluable to future engineers asking "why on earth did we do it this way?"

§6Style That Gets Read

  • Short, plain sentences and defined terms. Clear writing signals clear thinking.
  • Structure for skimming: headings, a summary, tables, and diagrams; put the conclusion first.
  • Write for the reader's context, not yours: someone from another team, six months from now, should be able to follow it.
  • Keep it as short as the decision allows. Two clear pages beat ten padded ones.
The document is not the goal The goal is a good decision that people understand and support. A design doc is a tool for getting there: it should change when good objections arrive, and a proposal that survives honest scrutiny is stronger than one that was never challenged.

§7Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Jumping to the solution without evidence of the problemReviewers debate the solution without agreeing a problem existsOpen with the problem, impact, and data
Omitting or straw-manning alternativesSkeptics find the gap immediately; trust dropsPresent real alternatives fairly and explain the trade-offs
Sharing the document late, or with no decision processFeedback arrives too late to matter, and discussion driftsCirculate early, name reviewers and a decider, and set a date
Practice
  1. Write a two-page design doc for a real change you would like to make (use the template above), including at least two serious alternatives.
  2. Give it to a colleague and ask them to play the skeptical reviewer. List their questions and add the answers to the doc.
  3. Take a past decision your team made and write the missing "alternatives and why we rejected them" section as a decision record.
Key takeaways
  • Design docs make thinking reviewable: use them for hard-to-reverse, cross-team, or contested changes.
  • Lead with the problem and evidence, state goals and non-goals, compare real alternatives, and be candid about risks.
  • Write for a skeptical reader, circulate early, set a decider and a date, and record the outcome and reasoning.
