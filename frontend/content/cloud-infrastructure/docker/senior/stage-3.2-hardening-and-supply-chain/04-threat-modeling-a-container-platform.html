Threat Modeling a Container Platform

Threat modeling maps the platform's assets, entry points, and trust boundaries so you spend effort on the controls that stop realistic attacks.

§1Start With Assets and Boundaries

List what you must protect (customer data, credentials, source code, production availability), then draw the system with its trust boundaries: internet to edge, edge to application, application to data, container to host, CI to registry to runtime, and developer laptop to everything. Threats cluster where data crosses a boundary or privilege changes.

§2Walking the STRIDE Categories

A simple structure is STRIDE: Spoofing, Tampering, Repudiation, Information disclosure, Denial of service, Elevation of privilege. Applied to a container platform:

AreaExample threatLikely control
Image supply chainA malicious or vulnerable base image or dependencyPinned digests, scanning, signing, trusted registries
RegistryOverwritten tag, stolen push credentialsImmutable tags, scoped short-lived tokens, audit logs
CI/CDPipeline compromise ships a backdoorProtected branches, ephemeral runners, least-privilege tokens, review
RuntimeApp vulnerability gives code executionNon-root, read-only FS, dropped capabilities, seccomp
HostEscape via kernel, runtime, or mounted socketPatching, no privileged or socket mounts, user namespaces
SecretsCredentials in env vars, logs, or imagesShort-lived file-based secrets, redaction, rotation
NetworkLateral movement between servicesSegmented networks, internal networks, minimal publishing
ResourcesOne tenant exhausts the hostMemory, CPU, and PID limits; per-service quotas
Daemon APIExposed TCP socket gives rootNever expose without TLS auth; prefer SSH contexts

§3Prioritizing Controls

For each threat estimate likelihood and impact, then rank controls by risk reduced per unit of effort. For many teams the highest-value, lowest-cost controls are the same few: run as non-root with dropped capabilities, keep the Docker socket and privileged mode out of workloads, scan and rebuild base images regularly, scope and shorten credentials, restrict registry and CI permissions, patch hosts, and segment networks. Rank by evidence, such as past incidents and scan data, not by what sounds worst.

# A lightweight written record per threat
Threat:    Stolen registry push token overwrites prod tag
Impact:    High (arbitrary code in production)
Likelihood: Medium
Controls:  Immutable tags; deploy by digest; OIDC short-lived tokens
Owner:     platform team          Review: 2026-Q3
A threat model is a living document Redraw the boundaries when architecture changes, and revisit it after incidents and near misses. A model written once and filed away is only paperwork.

§4Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Focusing only on exotic kernel escapesMisconfigurations and leaked credentials are more commonRank by likelihood and impact, not novelty
Modeling only the runtimeCI and registry compromise bypass every runtime controlInclude the whole supply chain
No owners for controlsControls decay unnoticedAssign each control an owner and review date
Practice
  1. Draw the trust boundaries for a small platform you know and list three threats crossing each one.
  2. Pick your top five controls and justify each with the threat it addresses and its cost.
Key takeaways
  • Model assets, boundaries, and entry points before choosing controls.
  • Use a structure such as STRIDE to avoid blind spots, and cover CI and registry as well as runtime.
  • Prioritize by risk reduced per effort, and keep the model current.
