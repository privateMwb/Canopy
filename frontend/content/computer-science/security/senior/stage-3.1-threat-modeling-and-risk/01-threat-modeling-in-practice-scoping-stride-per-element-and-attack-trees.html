Threat Modeling in Practice: Scoping, STRIDE per Element & Attack Trees

A real threat-modeling session is a short, structured conversation that scopes a system, finds threats systematically, and ends with decisions, not a long document nobody reads.

§1Four Questions

A widely used framing, popularized by Adam Shostack, keeps a session on track:

  1. What are we working on? Draw the data-flow diagram and mark trust boundaries.
  2. What can go wrong? Enumerate threats against each element.
  3. What are we going to do about it? Choose and assign a response for each.
  4. Did we do a good job? Check coverage, and revisit after changes.

Scope tightly: one feature or one service per session. Invite the people who built it, someone from operations, and a security engineer. Developers know where the system departs from the diagram.

§2STRIDE per Element

Rather than asking all six STRIDE questions of everything, apply the ones that make sense for each diagram element.

ElementTypical threats
External entity (a user, another system)Spoofing, Repudiation
Process (your code)All six: S, T, R, I, D, E
Data flowTampering, Information disclosure, Denial of service
Data storeTampering, Information disclosure, Denial of service (and Repudiation if it holds logs)

Threats concentrate on flows that cross a trust boundary, so spend the most time there.

§3Attack Trees

An attack tree starts from an attacker's goal and breaks it into the ways to achieve it. OR nodes are alternatives; AND nodes need all children. Annotating leaves with cost or difficulty shows the cheapest path, which is where defenses pay off most.

Goal: read another user's invoices
 ├─ OR: guess or enumerate invoice IDs        (cheap)
 ├─ OR: steal a session
 │    ├─ XSS on the invoice page             (medium)
 │    └─ phish the user                       (medium)
 └─ OR: compromise the database               (expensive)

§4The Output

End with a ranked list: each threat, the chosen response (mitigate, eliminate, transfer, or accept), an owner, and a due date or ticket. Track the accepted risks explicitly, with a name beside each acceptance, so they're decisions someone made and not omissions.

From the Junior stage This is the same process as Stage 1.1, scaled up. What changes at Senior level is facilitation: keeping scope tight, getting honest input from builders, and turning findings into tracked work.

§5Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Modeling the whole company in one sessionShallow findings and nobody follows upScope to one feature or service and repeat
Excluding the engineers who built itThe diagram doesn't match realityInclude builders and operators, and verify against the real system
Producing a document with no ownersThreats are noted but never fixedTurn every threat into a tracked item with an owner
Practice
  1. Run a 45-minute threat-modeling session on a feature you know with at least one other person, and record the four questions' answers.
  2. Draw an attack tree for stealing a user's session, and mark the cheapest path.
Key takeaways
  • Run sessions around four questions and keep the scope to one feature or service.
  • Apply STRIDE per diagram element and focus on flows that cross trust boundaries.
  • Finish with ranked, owned decisions, including explicitly accepted risks.
