Explaining Risk to Engineers & Executives

A finding that nobody acts on has the same value as one that was never found, so the senior skill is writing and presenting risk so that the right people decide the right thing.

§1Anatomy of a Good Finding

PartWhat it says
TitleThe problem, in plain words ("Any user can read other customers' invoices")
ImpactWhat an attacker gains, in terms the reader cares about
Likelihood and evidenceHow hard it is to exploit and how you know
FixA specific, realistic remediation, and a cheaper stopgap if there is one
Severity and priorityYour rating and the reasoning
UncertaintyWhat you didn't verify or don't know

Include a minimal reproduction so an engineer can see it. Describe impact without drama and without understating it.

§2Two Audiences

EngineersExecutives
WantWhat's wrong, where, and how to fix itWhat's at stake, how much, and what decision is needed
LanguagePrecise and technical; code and stepsBusiness terms: customers, revenue, legal exposure, time
FormatTicket with reproduction and fixOne page: top risks, options with costs, recommendation

Lead with the conclusion and the decision you need, then the reasoning. For leadership, present options with tradeoffs (fix now at cost X, mitigate for Y, accept with these consequences) and a clear recommendation.

§3Honest Uncertainty and Trust

  • Say what you know and what you don't, and how you'd find out. Overstated certainty damages credibility the first time you're wrong.
  • Don't use fear. Credible, specific impact persuades more than alarm, and crying wolf spends trust you'll need later.
  • Credit the people who built it and frame problems as shared work. Blame makes people hide issues.
  • Follow up: track the fix to closure and report the result.
A test for your write-up Can the reader, with no other context, say what could happen, how likely it is, what you recommend, and what happens if they do nothing? If not, rewrite it.

§4Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Reporting technical detail with no business impactLeaders can't prioritize itTranslate impact into customers, money, and obligations
Overstating certainty or severityCredibility drops when reality differsState confidence and unknowns plainly
Presenting a problem without optionsLeadership can't decideOffer two or three options with costs and a recommendation
Practice
  1. Rewrite a technical finding (for example a missing authorization check) as an engineer ticket and as a three-sentence executive summary.
  2. Take a past incident or bug and write the options-and-recommendation page you'd give leadership.
Key takeaways
  • A good finding states impact, evidence, a concrete fix, and what's uncertain.
  • Engineers need reproduction and fixes; executives need stakes, options, and a recommendation.
  • Be honest about uncertainty, avoid fear, and follow the finding through to closure.
