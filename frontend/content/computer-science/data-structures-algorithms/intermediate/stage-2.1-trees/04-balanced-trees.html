Balanced Trees

Why an unbalanced BST degrades to a linked list, and how self-balancing trees prevent it.

§1Recapping the Problem

From the BST lesson: inserting sorted data into a plain BST produces a degenerate shape with O(n) operations instead of O(log n). Self-balancing trees fix this by automatically restructuring themselves whenever an insert or delete would make them too lopsided.

§2AVL Trees — Height-Balanced

An AVL tree enforces that, for every node, the heights of its left and right subtrees differ by at most 1. Whenever an insertion or deletion would violate this, the tree performs rotations — local restructuring operations that restore balance without breaking the BST ordering property.

§3Red-Black Trees — A Looser, Faster-to-Maintain Balance

Red-black trees allow slightly more imbalance than AVL trees (in exchange for needing fewer rotations on average), using a coloring scheme (each node red or black) with rules that guarantee the tree's height never exceeds roughly 2x the minimum possible. This makes them common in practice — many language standard libraries' ordered map/set implementations use red-black trees internally.

§4AVL vs. Red-Black — the Practical Tradeoff

AVLRed-Black
Balance strictnessStricter (height diff ≤ 1)Looser

§5The Key Takeaway, Without the Rotation Details

What actually matters here You rarely implement a self-balancing tree from scratch in practice — standard libraries provide them. What matters is knowing why they exist (guaranteeing O(log n) regardless of insertion order) and recognizing when a plain BST isn't good enough for the job.

§6Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Using a plain BST when insertion order isn't controlled/randomRisk of degenerate O(n) performanceUse a self-balancing tree (or a language's built-in ordered structure) when this risk matters
Assuming AVL and red-black trees perform identicallyMissing the real lookup-vs-update tradeoff between themChoose based on whether the workload is more lookup-heavy or update-heavy
Practice
  1. Explain, in your own words, why inserting sorted data breaks a plain BST but not a self-balancing one.
  2. Describe one real scenario where choosing AVL over red-black (or vice versa) would make sense, based on the lookup/update tradeoff.
Key takeaways
  • Self-balancing trees restructure themselves (via rotations) to guarantee O(log n) height, regardless of insertion order.
  • AVL trees balance more strictly (faster lookups); red-black trees balance more loosely (faster updates).
  • In practice, you use a library's self-balancing tree rather than implementing one — knowing why they exist matters more than the rotation mechanics.
