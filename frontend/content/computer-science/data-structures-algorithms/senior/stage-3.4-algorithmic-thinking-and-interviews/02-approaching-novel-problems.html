Approaching Novel Problems

A repeatable process for problems you've never seen before: clarify, brute force, optimize.

§1Why a Process Matters More Than Memorized Solutions

You can't memorize every possible problem. What transfers is a reliable process for approaching something genuinely unfamiliar — the same process experienced engineers use on real, never-before-seen problems, not just interview puzzles.

§2Step One: Clarify Before Solving

  • What are the actual input constraints? (Size, range of values, can it be empty?)
  • What should happen on edge cases — empty input, single element, duplicates?
  • Is there a specific time/space complexity target?
  • Restate the problem in your own words to confirm you understood it correctly.

Skipping this step is the single most common cause of solving the wrong problem efficiently.

§3Step Two: Get a Brute Force Working First

Why brute force first, even if it's slow A working O(n²) solution is more valuable than a half-finished attempt at the "optimal" approach. It confirms you understand the problem correctly, gives you a baseline to test optimized versions against, and is something concrete to improve from rather than abstract.

§4Step Three: Identify the Bottleneck, Then Optimize

Look at the brute force solution and ask specifically: what's the expensive part? Repeated work that could be cached (memoization)? A linear search that could be a hash lookup? Nested loops that could become a single pass with the right data structure? This is where the pattern recognition from earlier lessons (two-pointer, sliding window, DP, etc.) gets applied — not from scratch, but by recognizing which known pattern matches the bottleneck you've identified.

§5Step Four: Verify, Including Edge Cases

Trace the optimized solution against the same edge cases identified in step one. A solution that's fast but wrong is worse than one that's slow but correct — speed is only valuable once correctness is established.

§6Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Jumping straight to an "optimal" approach without a working brute forceGetting stuck with nothing concrete, and often solving a misunderstood version of the problemAlways get something correct working first, however slow
Skipping clarifying questionsSolving a problem that isn't quite the one actually being askedAlways confirm constraints and edge-case expectations before coding
Practice
  1. Pick an unfamiliar problem (any source) and write down clarifying questions you'd ask before coding.
  2. Write a brute-force solution first, then identify its specific bottleneck before attempting to optimize.
Key takeaways
  • A repeatable process — clarify, brute force, optimize, verify — transfers to problems you've never seen before.
  • A working brute-force solution is a valuable checkpoint, not a waste of time.
  • Optimization means identifying the actual bottleneck and matching it to a known pattern, not guessing.
