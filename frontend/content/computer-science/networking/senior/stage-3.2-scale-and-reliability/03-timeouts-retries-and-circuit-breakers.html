Timeouts, Retries & Circuit Breakers

In a chain of services, a slow dependency can sink everything upstream, and careful timeouts, retries, and breakers are what stop a small fault from becoming an outage.

§1Timeouts: Budget Your Time

A call without a timeout can wait forever, holding a thread, a connection, and memory while more requests pile up behind it. Always set timeouts, and distinguish them:

  • Connect timeout: how long to wait for the TCP and TLS handshake (short, such as 1 second)
  • Read/response timeout: how long to wait for data after connecting
  • Overall deadline: the total time the caller is willing to spend, including retries

Timeouts must nest. If the user-facing request has a 2-second budget, the services it calls need smaller timeouts, and calls in sequence must fit inside the total. Passing the remaining deadline downstream, as gRPC does, lets every hop stop wasting effort once the caller has given up.

§2Retries Without Retry Storms

Retrying turns transient errors, such as a dropped connection or a brief 503, into successes. But careless retries amplify outages. If each of three layers retries three times, one failure becomes 3 × 3 × 3 = 27 attempts against an already struggling service.

  • Retry only what's safe: idempotent requests, or ones with idempotency keys, and only on retryable errors (timeouts, connection resets, 503), never on 400-class errors
  • Back off exponentially with jitter so that clients spread out rather than retrying in lockstep
  • Cap attempts at two or three
  • Use a retry budget: allow retries to add at most about 10% extra traffic, and stop retrying when the budget is spent
  • Retry at one layer, usually the edge or the client, not at every hop
import random, time
def backoff(attempt, base=0.1, cap=5.0):
    return random.uniform(0, min(cap, base * 2 ** attempt))   # 'full jitter'

for attempt in range(3):
    try:
        return call_service(timeout=1.0)
    except RetryableError:
        time.sleep(backoff(attempt))
raise

§3Circuit Breakers and Bulkheads

When a dependency is clearly failing, continuing to call it wastes resources and delays the caller. A circuit breaker tracks recent failures and trips:

StateBehavior
ClosedCalls go through normally; failures are counted
OpenCalls fail immediately without reaching the dependency, for a cooldown period
Half-openA few trial calls test recovery; success closes the circuit, failure reopens it

Failing fast frees resources and gives the dependency room to recover. Pair it with a fallback: a cached value, a default, or a degraded feature. Bulkheads isolate resources, such as separate connection pools or thread limits per dependency, so that one slow service can't use up capacity needed by the others.

Hedged requests reduce tail latency for idempotent reads: if a response hasn't arrived by the 95th-percentile time, send a duplicate to another replica and take whichever answers first, at the cost of some extra load.

The key insight Most large outages are cascades: a slowdown triggers retries, retries add load, and load deepens the slowdown. Timeouts, jittered backoff, retry budgets, and circuit breakers are the controls that break that loop.

§4Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
No timeout on outbound callsThreads and connections pile up behind one slow dependencySet connect, read, and overall timeouts everywhere
Retrying at every layerExponential amplification during an incidentRetry at one layer, with a budget
Retrying without jitterAll clients retry at the same momentUse exponential backoff with random jitter
Practice
  1. Take a three-service call chain with a 1-second user-facing budget, and assign timeouts and retry counts to each hop.
  2. Implement a simple circuit breaker with closed, open, and half-open states, and test it against a service that fails 80% of the time.
Key takeaways
  • Set connect, read, and overall timeouts, and nest them within the caller's deadline.
  • Retry only safe operations, with jittered exponential backoff, a small cap, and a budget.
  • Circuit breakers and bulkheads stop failures from cascading and give dependencies room to recover.
