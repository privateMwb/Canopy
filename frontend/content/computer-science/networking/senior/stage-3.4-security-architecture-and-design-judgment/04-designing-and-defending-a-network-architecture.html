Designing and Defending a Network Architecture

A good design isn't just a diagram that works; it's a set of choices whose tradeoffs you can state, justify, and revisit when the facts change.

§1Start With Requirements

Before drawing boxes, write down what the system must do and how well. Vague goals produce arbitrary designs.

AskExample answers
Who are the clients, and where?Mobile apps worldwide; internal services; partners
What are the traffic patterns?Read-heavy, bursty at 9 a.m.; 2,000 requests per second peak
What are the availability and latency targets?99.95% monthly; p99 under 300 ms
What data is sensitive?Payment data, personal data, regulated records
What are the constraints?Budget, team size, compliance, existing systems

Requirements also tell you what *not* to build. A 50-request-per-second internal tool doesn't need multi-region active-active failover.

§2Make Tradeoffs Explicit

For each significant decision, list the realistic options and what each gains and costs, then record why you chose one. A short decision record is enough.

Decision: Load balancing for the public API
Context:  ~2k rps peak, REST + a few WebSocket endpoints, 3 engineers on call
Options:
  A. Cloud L7 load balancer       + managed, TLS termination, WebSocket support
                                  - per-request cost, less routing flexibility
  B. Self-managed nginx fleet     + full control   - we must patch and operate it
Decision: A.   Revisit if: cost exceeds budget, or we need custom routing logic.
Risks:    vendor limits on idle WebSocket timeouts → mitigated with 30 s heartbeats.

The 'revisit if' line is what makes a decision defensible later. It says what evidence would change your mind, so a change of direction is a planned response rather than an admission of error.

§3Design for Failure and Explain It

Walk the design through failures in order of likelihood: an instance dies, an availability zone fails, a dependency slows down, a certificate expires, a deploy goes wrong, a client misbehaves. For each, name what detects it, what limits the impact, and how recovery happens, tying back to the tools you know: health checks and draining, timeouts and retry budgets, circuit breakers, rate limits, and segmentation.

  • Show the path of a request end to end: DNS, CDN, load balancer, service, database, naming where TLS terminates and where authentication happens
  • Identify single points of failure and either remove them or accept them knowingly
  • State the security model: what's public, what's internal, who can call what, and what a breach of each component exposes
  • Say what you measured versus assumed: assumptions are where designs fail

Adjust the explanation for the audience. Engineers want request paths, failure modes, and numbers. Executives and non-technical stakeholders want the risk, cost, and the decision in plain terms: 'If one data center fails, users see a brief slowdown rather than an outage, at about 30% higher cost.' Analogies help, but state the limits of the analogy.

When challenged, separate facts, assumptions, and preferences. Concede quickly when a challenge exposes a real gap, defend the choices you've reasoned through, and say 'I don't know yet, and here's how I'd find out' when that's true.

The key insight The strongest designs aren't the most elaborate. They match the requirements, show their tradeoffs, and say in advance what would make them change.

§4Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Designing for imagined scaleComplexity and cost with no benefitDesign for known requirements, with a clear path to grow
Presenting one option as the only optionReviewers can't tell whether alternatives were consideredShow the options you rejected and why
Leaving failure modes for laterOutages reveal them firstWalk through failures during design and record the mitigations
Practice
  1. Pick a service you know and write a one-page decision record for its load balancing or data-path choice, including a 'revisit if' condition.
  2. Explain the same architecture twice: once to an engineer in three minutes, and once to a non-technical stakeholder in three sentences.
Key takeaways
  • Start from requirements: clients, traffic, targets, sensitivity, and constraints.
  • Record decisions with options, tradeoffs, and a 'revisit if' trigger.
  • Walk through failures, show request paths, and tailor the explanation to the audience.
