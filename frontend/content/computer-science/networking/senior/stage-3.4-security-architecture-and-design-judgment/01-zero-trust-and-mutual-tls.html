Zero Trust & Mutual TLS

The old assumption that anything inside the firewall is safe fails once an attacker is inside, and zero trust replaces it with verifying every request.

§1Why the Perimeter Model Fails

Traditional security protected a network edge and trusted everything within it. But attackers get inside through phished credentials, a compromised laptop, a vulnerable service, or a malicious dependency, and once inside a flat network they can reach almost everything. Cloud services, remote work, and microservices have also dissolved the clear edge.

Zero trust assumes the network is hostile everywhere. Each request must prove who is making it, and be checked against policy, regardless of where it comes from. The common principles are:

  • Authenticate and authorize every request, from users and from services
  • Least privilege: grant the minimum access needed, for the minimum time
  • Assume breach: limit blast radius so one compromised component can't reach everything
  • Verify continuously, using device health, context, and short-lived credentials

§2Mutual TLS and Service Identity

In ordinary TLS, only the server proves its identity. In mutual TLS (mTLS), the client also presents a certificate, so both sides authenticate cryptographically. Services use it to know exactly which service is calling them, and not just which IP address.

# Client authenticating with its own certificate
curl https://orders.internal:8443/ --cert client.crt --key client.key --cacert internal-ca.crt

# nginx requiring a valid client certificate
ssl_client_certificate /etc/ssl/internal-ca.crt;
ssl_verify_client on;

Identity is in the certificate, often as a URI such as spiffe://prod/ns/payments/sa/api under the SPIFFE standard, rather than in a network address, which changes constantly in dynamic environments. Certificates are issued automatically by an internal CA and are short-lived, hours or days, so a stolen one soon becomes useless, and rotation is automated. Service meshes (Stage 3.2) commonly provide mTLS transparently.

§3Identity Is Not Authorization

mTLS proves *who* is calling. It doesn't decide *what they may do*. A policy layer must still say, for example, that the checkout service may call POST /payments on the payments service but the search service may not.

QuestionMechanism
Who is the caller?mTLS certificate or signed token
Is this caller allowed this operation?Authorization policy (per service, method, path)
Is the end user allowed this data?Per-object checks in the application, using the user's token

Costs include certificate issuance and rotation infrastructure, debugging harder-to-inspect encrypted internal traffic, and a latency overhead that's usually small with connection reuse. Adopt it incrementally, starting with the most sensitive paths, and monitor for expiring certificates, since an expired internal certificate causes outages just like a public one.

The key insight Zero trust doesn't mean 'trust nothing and do nothing'; it means every access decision is made explicitly, with strong identity and least privilege, instead of being granted implicitly by network location.

§4Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Encrypting internal traffic but allowing any clientEncryption without authentication doesn't stop an impostor serviceRequire client certificates and enforce authorization policy
Long-lived internal certificatesA leaked key works for yearsIssue short-lived certificates and automate rotation
Treating mTLS as the whole policyAny authenticated service can call any otherAdd per-service, per-method authorization rules
Practice
  1. Set up two local services where one requires a client certificate signed by your own test CA, and show that a request without one is rejected.
  2. Write an authorization policy in plain language for a payments service: which callers may do what.
Key takeaways
  • Zero trust verifies every request and limits blast radius, instead of trusting the internal network.
  • mTLS gives both sides cryptographic identity; short-lived, automatically rotated certificates limit exposure.
  • Identity must be paired with authorization policy that says what each caller may do.
