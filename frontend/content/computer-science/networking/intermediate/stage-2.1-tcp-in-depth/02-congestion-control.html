Congestion Control

Flow control protects the receiver; congestion control protects the network, and it works by probing for capacity and backing off when packets are lost.

§1The Congestion Window

A sender doesn't know how much capacity the path has, and sending too much overloads routers and causes loss for everyone. TCP keeps a second limit, the congestion window (cwnd), which it adjusts based on feedback. The amount in flight is capped by the smaller of cwnd and the receive window.

  • Slow start: cwnd starts small (10 segments on modern Linux) and roughly doubles every RTT until loss occurs or a threshold (ssthresh) is reached
  • Congestion avoidance: past the threshold, cwnd grows by about one segment per RTT, cautiously
  • On loss: cwnd is cut, classically halved. This pattern of additive increase and multiplicative decrease is AIMD
cwnd over time (segments):
 10 → 20 → 40 → 80 → 160  slow start: exponential
              loss!  → 80   cut in half
 80 → 81 → 82 → 83 ...     congestion avoidance: linear

§2Why a Short Connection Never Speeds Up

Slow start means every new connection begins slowly. A 100 KB response over a fresh connection may finish before cwnd ever grows large, so latency rather than bandwidth limits it. This is a major reason to reuse connections: a warm connection has a large cwnd already.

ss -ti dst 203.0.113.9
# cwnd:10 ssthresh:... rtt:48.2/12.1 retrans:0/2

Raising the initial window helps short transfers, which is why the default rose from 3 to 10 segments.

§3Algorithms

AlgorithmSignal it reacts toCharacter
Reno / NewRenoPacket lossClassic AIMD; slow to fill high-bandwidth, high-latency paths
CUBICPacket lossGrows by a cubic curve; the default on Linux; good on fast paths
BBRMeasured bandwidth and minimum RTTAims at the rate the path can carry without filling buffers; not driven by loss
sysctl net.ipv4.tcp_congestion_control       # cubic
sysctl net.ipv4.tcp_available_congestion_control

Loss-based algorithms have a side effect called bufferbloat: they keep growing until router buffers fill, adding queuing delay even though throughput looks fine. A rough guide to loss-limited throughput is the Mathis formula: rate ≈ (MSS / RTT) × 1.22 / √p, where p is the loss rate. With a 1460-byte MSS and 50 ms RTT, 0.01% loss allows about 3.6 MB/s, but 1% loss cuts that to about 0.36 MB/s.

The key insight On a lossy or high-latency path, TCP throughput is set by loss and RTT, not by the link's bandwidth. A 1 Gbps link with 1% loss and a 100 ms RTT delivers a small fraction of its capacity.

§4Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Expecting a new connection to use full bandwidth immediatelySlow start means early data is slowReuse connections; consider larger initial windows
Treating all loss as network failureCongestion control reacts to any loss, including wireless noiseOn lossy links, consider BBR or link-layer fixes
Judging a link by speed tests with parallel streams aloneA single flow may reach far lessTest the single-connection case your application actually uses
Practice
  1. Starting from cwnd=10, how many RTTs of slow start does it take to exceed 1,000 segments?
  2. Use the Mathis formula to estimate throughput at 0.1% loss and 80 ms RTT with a 1460-byte MSS.
Key takeaways
  • Slow start doubles cwnd per RTT; congestion avoidance adds about one segment per RTT; loss cuts it.
  • Loss and RTT, not bandwidth, usually limit a single TCP flow's speed.
  • CUBIC is the Linux default, and BBR uses measured bandwidth and RTT instead of loss.
