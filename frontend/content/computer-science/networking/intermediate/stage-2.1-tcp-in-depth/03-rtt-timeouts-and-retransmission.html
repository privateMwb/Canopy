RTT, Timeouts & Retransmission

TCP decides when to give up waiting by measuring round-trip times, and the difference between a fast retransmit and a timeout can be hundreds of milliseconds.

§1Measuring RTT and Setting the Timer

TCP samples the time between sending a segment and receiving its ACK, and keeps a smoothed average (SRTT) and a measure of variation (RTTVAR). The retransmission timeout is based on both.

RTO = SRTT + 4 * RTTVAR       # floor of 200 ms on Linux; initial RTO is 1 second

# Stable path: SRTT = 50 ms, RTTVAR = 5 ms  →  RTO ≈ 70 ms, raised to the 200 ms floor
# Jittery path: SRTT = 50 ms, RTTVAR = 40 ms →  RTO ≈ 210 ms

Setting it too low causes spurious retransmissions, resending data that wasn't lost. Setting it too high leaves the connection idle after a real loss. Following Karn's algorithm, TCP ignores RTT samples from retransmitted segments, since it can't tell which transmission the ACK refers to.

§2Fast Retransmit vs. Timeout

RecoveryTriggerCost
Fast retransmitThree duplicate ACKsAbout one RTT; cwnd is cut but the connection keeps flowing
Timeout (RTO)No ACK within the RTOAt least the RTO, often 200 ms to seconds; cwnd collapses to a minimum and slow start restarts

Timeouts are far more damaging than fast retransmits. If a lost segment is the last one sent, no later data produces duplicate ACKs, so only the timer can detect it. Modern stacks add tail loss probes, which send an extra segment shortly after the last one to provoke an ACK sooner.

§3Backoff and What You See

If a retransmission also gets no reply, the RTO doubles each time (exponential backoff): 200 ms, 400 ms, 800 ms, and so on. A connection that keeps failing eventually gives up after a limit (about 15 retries by default, roughly 15 minutes), and the application sees a timeout error.

ss -ti dst 203.0.113.9
# rtt:48/6 rto:200 retrans:0/3 ...   ← the second number counts total retransmissions
nstat -az TcpTimeouts TcpFastRetrans TcpRetransSegs

If TcpTimeouts climbs while TcpFastRetrans stays low, losses are hitting the tail of transfers or whole windows, a sign of drops, outages, or middlebox trouble rather than light congestion.

The key insight A single timeout can cost hundreds of milliseconds, while a fast retransmit costs about one RTT. Long-tail latency spikes are often retransmission timeouts in disguise.

§4Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Looking only at average RTTTimeout behavior depends on variation tooCheck jitter and RTTVAR alongside the mean
Ignoring a high timeout counterLatency spikes with no obvious causeCompare TcpTimeouts with TcpFastRetrans over time
Setting application timeouts below TCP's recovery timeRequests are abandoned just as TCP would have recoveredSet client timeouts with retransmission delays in mind
Practice
  1. In a capture, find a retransmitted segment. Was it triggered by duplicate ACKs or a timeout, and how long was the gap?
  2. If SRTT is 120 ms and RTTVAR is 30 ms, what's the RTO? What are the next two RTOs after consecutive timeouts?
Key takeaways
  • RTO is derived from smoothed RTT plus four times its variation, and doubles on each consecutive timeout.
  • Fast retransmit after three duplicate ACKs is cheap; a timeout is expensive.
  • Check ss -ti and nstat for retransmission and timeout counts when latency spikes.
