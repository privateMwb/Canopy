Incident Response & Blameless Postmortems

Incidents are inevitable, so reliability comes from responding in a calm and organized way, then learning from each one without blaming a person.

§1Running an Incident

Separate roles so people do not all debug at once. The incident commander coordinates and decides, a communications lead updates stakeholders, and responders investigate. Mitigate first, diagnose later: rolling back or failing over to restore service is better than hunting the root cause while users suffer.

# the first 15 minutes
1. Declare the incident, and name an incident commander
2. Open one channel and one shared timeline (who did what, when)
3. Assess impact: who is affected and how badly?
4. Mitigate: roll back, fail over, disable the feature flag, scale up
5. Communicate on a fixed cadence, such as every 30 minutes, even if "no change"
6. Confirm recovery from user-facing metrics, not just "the deploy finished"

§2Communicating Under Pressure

Say what you know, what you do not know, and when the next update comes. Stakeholders can tolerate bad news, but not silence. Keep decisions and actions in the shared channel so a new responder can catch up in a minute.

[10:42] STATUS: Elevated checkout errors (about 8%) since 10:15 UTC.
[10:42] IMPACT: Some customers cannot complete payment.
[10:42] ACTION: Rolled back release 1.4.3; monitoring recovery.
[10:42] NEXT UPDATE: 11:12 UTC, or sooner if the situation changes.

§3Blameless Postmortems

A postmortem explains what happened and how to make it less likely or less harmful next time. Blameless does not mean no accountability. It means we assume people acted reasonably with the information they had, and we look for the systemic causes: missing safeguards, unclear signals, and risky defaults. Individuals who fear blame hide facts, and then you lose the learning.

# postmortem outline
Summary:        2-3 sentences on what happened and the impact
Timeline:       key events with timestamps (detection, mitigation, recovery)
Root causes:    contributing factors, asking "why" repeatedly, not "who"
What went well: what limited the damage
Action items:   specific, owned, dated: "add a canary stage" not "be careful"
Action items are the point A postmortem without tracked, prioritized follow-ups is just a story. Measure whether the actions get done, and watch for repeating themes across incidents, which show the weak places in the system.

§4Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Debugging root cause before restoring serviceUsers stay down longer than neededMitigate first, then investigate
Naming a person as the causePeople hide mistakes, and the system problem staysAsk what let the mistake reach production and why it was not caught
Vague action items like "be more careful"Nothing changesWrite specific, owned, dated engineering changes
Practice
  1. Write a blameless postmortem for a real or invented outage, with a timeline and three owned action items.
  2. Draft a status update that states impact, action, and the time of the next update.
Key takeaways
  • Assign incident roles, mitigate first, and communicate on a regular cadence.
  • Blameless postmortems look for systemic causes rather than a person to blame.
  • Follow-up actions are the real output, and they must be owned and tracked.
