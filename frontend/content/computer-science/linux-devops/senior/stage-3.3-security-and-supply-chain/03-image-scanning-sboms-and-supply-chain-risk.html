Image Scanning, SBOMs & Supply-Chain Risk

Most of what you run was written by someone else, so you need to know what is inside your images and be able to prove where they came from.

§1Scanning for Known Vulnerabilities

Images contain an operating system layer and language dependencies, and both can have known vulnerabilities (CVEs). A scanner compares what is installed with vulnerability databases. Run it in CI and fail the build above a chosen severity, but expect noise, since not every finding is reachable or exploitable.

trivy image --severity HIGH,CRITICAL --exit-code 1 ghcr.io/acme/shop:1.4.2
trivy fs --scanners vuln,secret .          # scan the source tree too
# triage: is it reachable? is a fix available? can the package be removed?

The most effective fix is often shrinking the image. A minimal or distroless base removes hundreds of packages you never use, along with their future vulnerabilities.

§2SBOMs: Knowing What You Ship

A software bill of materials (SBOM) lists every component in an artifact. When a new vulnerability such as a famous logging-library flaw is announced, an SBOM stored per release lets you answer "are we affected, and where?" in minutes instead of days.

syft ghcr.io/acme/shop:1.4.2 -o spdx-json > sbom.spdx.json
grype sbom:sbom.spdx.json                  # scan the SBOM later, as CVEs appear
jq '.packages | length' sbom.spdx.json     # how many components are inside?

§3Provenance and Signing

Even a clean image is dangerous if it was tampered with or did not come from your pipeline. Signing attaches a verifiable signature to an image, and provenance records how it was built: which source commit, which workflow, which builder. Pin dependencies and base images by digest, and verify signatures before deploying.

cosign sign --yes ghcr.io/acme/shop@sha256:9a1f...c4
cosign verify \
  --certificate-identity-regexp "https://github.com/acme/shop/.github/workflows/.*" \
  --certificate-oidc-issuer https://token.actions.githubusercontent.com \
  ghcr.io/acme/shop@sha256:9a1f...c4

Keyless signing ties the signature to the pipeline's OIDC identity, so there is no private key to manage or leak. A cluster admission policy can then refuse to run any image whose signature does not verify.

Trust, but verify Supply-chain attacks target what you pull and what you build with: dependencies, base images, CI actions. The defenses are boring and effective: pin versions, minimize what you include, record what you shipped, and verify what you run.

§4Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Scanning once and never againNew CVEs are published daily against old imagesScan in CI and on a schedule, and rebuild base images regularly
Pulling unpinned latest bases and dependenciesA compromised upstream release lands silentlyPin by version and digest, and update deliberately
Treating every scanner finding as an emergencyTeams drown in noise and ignore the scannerTriage by severity, reachability, and available fixes
Practice
  1. Scan one of your images, remove the largest source of findings by switching to a smaller base, and rescan.
  2. Generate an SBOM for a release and use it to answer "do we ship this library?"
Key takeaways
  • Scan images in CI, triage findings, and cut risk by shrinking what is inside.
  • An SBOM per release lets you answer "are we affected?" quickly.
  • Sign images, record provenance, pin by digest, and verify before running.
