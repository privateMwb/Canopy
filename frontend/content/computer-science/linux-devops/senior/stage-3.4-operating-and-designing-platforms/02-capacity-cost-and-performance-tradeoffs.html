Capacity, Cost & Performance Tradeoffs

Every reliability and performance decision is also a cost decision, so seniors reason about the tradeoff in numbers instead of defaulting to "more".

§1Capacity and Headroom

Systems do not degrade smoothly. As utilization climbs, queueing makes latency rise sharply, so a service at 90 percent CPU is typically much slower than one at 60. Plan headroom for traffic spikes, deploys (which temporarily run extra pods), and the loss of a node or zone ("N+1"). Find real limits by load testing, not by guessing.

# a load test answers: "at what request rate does p99 cross the SLO?"
k6 run --vus 200 --duration 10m checkout-test.js
# note the knee of the curve, then set autoscaling and headroom below it
# capacity rule of thumb: survive the loss of one zone at peak traffic

§2Right-Sizing and Autoscaling Economics

Over-provisioned requests are the most common waste, because you pay for reserved capacity, not what is used. Compare requests to actual usage and adjust. Autoscaling saves money only if scale-down is safe and fast, and stateless services scale far more easily than databases.

kubectl top pods --containers
# compare with the requests in the Deployment:
#   requests: cpu 1000m, memory 2Gi   actual p95: cpu 150m, memory 600Mi
# a 5x over-request is paying for four idle cores per replica

§3Where the Money Goes

Look for the big levers. Commitments (reserved or savings plans) cut the price of steady baseline load. Spot or preemptible instances are cheap but can vanish, so use them for stateless or batch work that tolerates interruption. Data transfer between zones and out to the internet, idle resources, and unbounded log or metric volume are frequent surprises. Tag everything so cost maps to a team and service.

# build an unambiguous number: unit economics
monthly infra cost            $48,000
requests per month            1.2 billion
cost per 1,000 requests       $0.04
# compare options by this number, not by the raw bill
# and compare the price of an extra nine of availability with the revenue it protects
Spend reliability where it matters Not every service needs three zones and 99.99 percent. Match investment to business impact: critical paths get redundancy and headroom, and internal tools can be cheaper and less available. Say the tradeoff explicitly so the business can choose it.

§4Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Running at high utilization with no headroomLatency spikes and no room for deploys or failuresKeep headroom, and load test to find the real knee
Never reviewing requests against usagePaying for capacity nobody usesRight-size regularly from measured percentiles
Optimizing the bill without unit costsSavings that hurt reliability or growth go unnoticedTrack cost per unit of work alongside the SLOs
Practice
  1. Compare requests with real p95 usage for one service, and estimate the monthly saving from right-sizing.
  2. Load test a service, find where p99 crosses its SLO, and set a headroom target from the result.
Key takeaways
  • Latency rises sharply near full utilization, so plan headroom and test to find real limits.
  • Right-size requests from measured usage, and use commitments and spot capacity for the right workloads.
  • Express cost as a unit economics number, and spend reliability where the business needs it.
