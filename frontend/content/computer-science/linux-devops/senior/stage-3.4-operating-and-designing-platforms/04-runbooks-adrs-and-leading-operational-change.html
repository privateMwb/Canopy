Runbooks, ADRs & Leading Operational Change

Writing down procedures and decisions, and persuading people to adopt them, is the part of senior operations work that scales beyond what you can do yourself.

§1Runbooks That Get Used

A runbook is a step-by-step guide for a specific alert or task, written for an engineer who is tired, stressed, and not the expert. Start from the symptom, give copy-pasteable commands, say what "good" looks like, and say when to escalate. Test them: a runbook nobody has followed is a guess.

# Runbook: CheckoutHighErrorRate
Impact:      customers cannot pay
Check first: dashboard link; did a deploy happen in the last hour?
Step 1:      kubectl rollout history deploy/checkout -n prod
Step 2:      if a recent release correlates, run:  kubectl rollout undo deploy/checkout -n prod
Expected:    error ratio falls below 1% within 5 minutes
If not:      page the payments on-call; check the database dashboard
Owner/last tested: payments-team / 2026-09-30

§2Architecture Decision Records

An ADR is a short document that records one significant decision: the context, the options considered, what was chosen, and the consequences. Future engineers can then understand why, instead of guessing or repeating the debate. Keep them in the repository, number them, and never edit old ones: supersede them with a new record.

# ADR-014: Use GitOps (Argo CD) for production deploys
Status:       Accepted (supersedes ADR-009)
Context:      Deploys run from CI with broad cluster credentials; no drift detection.
Options:      (A) keep push-based CI deploys  (B) Argo CD  (C) Flux
Decision:     Adopt Argo CD; the cluster pulls from the config repo.
Consequences: + audit trail, drift correction, no CI cluster credentials
              - new component to operate; teams must learn the config-repo flow

§3Leading Change

Good ideas fail without adoption. Write a proposal that states the problem and evidence, offer a small pilot with a willing team, show measured results, then expand. Communicate risk plainly: what could go wrong, how likely it is, how you will detect it, and how you would roll back. Listen for the objections, since they often hold the real constraints.

# a proposal that earns trust
1. Problem, with data    "34% of deploys need a manual hotfix; median restore 52 min"
2. Proposal and scope    "pilot canary releases on the checkout service for 6 weeks"
3. Risks and mitigations "extra capacity ~5%; auto-rollback on SLO burn"
4. Success criteria      "change failure rate under 10%; restore under 15 min"
5. Decision and owner    who decides, by when, who owns it afterwards
Clarity scales, heroics do not If only one person knows how to recover the system, that is a risk, not a strength. Runbooks, ADRs, and clear proposals turn private knowledge into shared capability and let a team operate without you.

§4Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Writing runbooks nobody has testedSteps fail or are missing during a real incidentRehearse them in game days, and record the last-tested date
Making decisions in chat and losing themThe reasoning is gone and debates repeatRecord significant decisions as ADRs in the repository
Pushing a change without a pilot or evidenceResistance, and no way to show it workedPilot with a willing team and measure before and after
Practice
  1. Write a runbook for one real alert and have a teammate follow it without your help; fix every place they got stuck.
  2. Write an ADR for a decision your team made recently, including the options you rejected and why.
Key takeaways
  • Runbooks should be symptom-driven, copy-pasteable, tested, and owned.
  • ADRs preserve the context and reasoning behind decisions, so they are not relitigated or lost.
  • Lead change with evidence, a small pilot, honest risk communication, and measurable success criteria.
