Designing and Defending a Data Architecture

A data architecture is a set of trade-offs made visible. Senior engineers can state requirements in numbers, propose a design, say what it costs, and explain it to people who don't speak database.

§1Start with Requirements, in Numbers

Every good design starts by turning vague wishes into measurable requirements.

  • Workload: reads/sec, writes/sec, peak vs. average, read:write ratio, growth rate.
  • Data: size now and in 3 years, record shapes, hot vs. cold data.
  • Latency: targets by operation (p99 under 100 ms for checkout reads).
  • Consistency: which operations need the latest data, and which can be stale?
  • Availability and durability: RTO and RPO, acceptable downtime, region failure tolerance.
  • Query patterns: the top 10 queries, plus the reporting and analytics needs.
  • Constraints: team skills, budget, compliance, existing systems, time to ship.

If you don't have numbers, estimate them and say so, and design for the order of magnitude, not false precision.

§3Writing the Design Document

A short, clear document (1–3 pages) beats a long one. A reliable structure:

  1. Context and goals: what problem, for whom, and what success looks like.
  2. Requirements and numbers: from above, including non-goals.
  3. Proposed design: a diagram, the data model, and the main data flows.
  4. Alternatives considered: and why each was not chosen.
  5. Trade-offs and risks: what this design is bad at; what could go wrong; how you'd detect it.
  6. Failure and recovery: what happens when a node, zone, or region fails; backup and restore plan.
  7. Migration and rollout: how to get there from here, safely, in stages.
  8. Open questions and decisions needed.
  9. Evolution: what signals would make you revisit this (for example "if writes exceed 5k/s, begin sharding by tenant").

§4Defending It to Different Audiences

  • To engineers: lead with requirements, show the numbers and the alternatives, invite attack on the assumptions, and be explicit about the weakest part of the design.
  • To product and business: translate into outcomes: "Customers can't lose a paid order; the system survives losing a data center; we can launch in the EU in Q3." Express cost as money and risk as likelihood × impact. Avoid jargon; offer an analogy only if it holds up.
  • To leadership: three things: the decision, the cost (and cost of delay), and the main risk with its mitigation.

In any room, separate facts from opinions, say "I don't know yet, here's how I'll find out" when true, and prefer reversible decisions: choose the option that leaves the most future choices open at an acceptable cost. Record decisions in short architecture decision records (ADRs) so future engineers know why, not just what.

The key insight A design isn't senior because it's sophisticated. It's senior because the requirements are explicit, the alternatives were weighed, the costs are stated honestly, and the plan says what would change the decision.

§5Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Designing for imagined scale ("millions of users") without numbersOver-engineering, cost, and slow deliveryEstimate real load and growth; design for 10× and note the path beyond
Presenting one option as the only optionNo one can judge the trade-offs, and objections arrive lateShow alternatives considered and why they lost
Omitting failure, recovery, and migration from the designBeautiful steady-state designs that can't be operated or adoptedInclude failure modes, backups, and a staged rollout
Explaining to non-engineers with technical detailDecision-makers disengage or decide blindlyFrame in outcomes, cost, and risk; keep detail on request
Practice
  1. Given: a marketplace with 500k users, 200 orders/min at peak (3× on sale days), 5-year growth to 5×, a 99.95% availability target, EU data residency, and a 5-person team. Write requirements in numbers and propose a data architecture in one page.
  2. Present your design aloud twice: once for engineers (3 minutes) and once for the CEO (90 seconds). What did you remove, and what did you add, for each audience?
Key takeaways
  • Start from measurable requirements: workload, data size, latency, consistency, availability, and constraints.
  • Compare real alternatives, state the costs and risks, and describe failure, recovery, and migration.
  • Tailor the explanation to the audience, favor reversible decisions, and record them in ADRs.
