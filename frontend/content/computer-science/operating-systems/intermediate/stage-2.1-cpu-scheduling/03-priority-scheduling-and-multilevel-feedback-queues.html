Priority Scheduling & Multilevel Feedback Queues

Priorities let important work jump ahead, and multilevel feedback queues learn which jobs are short without being told.

§1Priority Scheduling

Each job has a priority; the scheduler always runs the highest-priority ready job, with equal priorities typically sharing via Round Robin. The risk is starvation: a steady supply of high-priority work means low-priority jobs may never run.

The usual fix is aging: gradually raise a waiting job's priority until it eventually gets the CPU.

# Linux: nice values run from -20 (highest priority) to 19 (lowest)
nice -n 10 ./batch-job          # start at lower priority
renice -n 5 -p 1234             # lower an existing process's priority

§2The Multilevel Feedback Queue

MLFQ keeps several queues at different priority levels and moves jobs between them based on observed behavior. The rules, in their classic form:

  1. A higher-priority queue always runs before a lower one
  2. Jobs at the same level share the CPU with Round Robin
  3. A new job starts at the top level
  4. A job that uses up its time allotment at a level is demoted one level
  5. Periodically, every job is boosted back to the top

Short, interactive jobs usually finish or block before using their allotment, so they stay at high priority and respond quickly. Long CPU-bound jobs sink to lower levels, where they run in larger chunks without hurting responsiveness.

§3Why the Boost Matters

Without the periodic boost, a long job that has sunk to the bottom could starve under a stream of interactive jobs. The boost also helps a job that changes behavior, such as a compute-heavy phase followed by an interactive one.

# Gaming risk: a job can issue a tiny I/O just before its allotment ends,
# resetting its usage and staying at high priority.
# Fix: track total CPU time used at a level, not just per-slice behavior.

MLFQ-style designs underlie schedulers in several operating systems. It's a way of approximating SJF without prior knowledge of job lengths.

Gotcha A priority scheduler without aging or boosting isn't merely unfair; it can leave jobs unable to ever finish. Any priority design must answer 'what stops starvation?'

§4Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Using priorities without agingLow-priority jobs starve indefinitelyAdd aging or a periodic priority boost
Resetting a job's allotment whenever it does I/OA job games the scheduler by issuing tiny I/O to stay at the topAccount for total time used at each level
Assuming nice only affects the order of executionIt changes the share of CPU a process receivesUse it to adjust how much CPU a job gets, not strict ordering
Practice
  1. Simulate an MLFQ with three levels and quanta of 2, 4, and 8 for one long CPU-bound job and three short jobs. When does the long job sink?
  2. Explain how you would modify MLFQ so a job can't game it with many tiny I/O calls.
Key takeaways
  • Strict priority scheduling can starve low-priority jobs; aging or boosting prevents it.
  • MLFQ promotes interactive jobs and demotes CPU-bound ones based on observed behavior.
  • The periodic boost handles starvation and jobs that change behavior.
