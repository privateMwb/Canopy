Overfitting vs. Underfitting

The two failure modes every model can fall into, shown side by side with a simple example.

§1Two Ways to Fail

Underfitting: the model is too simple to capture the real pattern — it performs poorly even on training data. Overfitting: the model is too complex and memorizes the training data's noise — it performs great on training data but poorly on new data.

§2Spotting Each One

Train scoreTest score
UnderfittingLowLow
Good fitHighHigh (close to train)
OverfittingVery highNoticeably lower

§3A Concrete Example

Imagine fitting a curve to 10 noisy data points. A straight line might miss the real trend entirely (underfit). A wiggly curve that passes through every single point exactly has memorized the noise, not the pattern (overfit). The right complexity sits between the two.

§4What Causes Each

  • Underfitting causes: model too simple for the problem, too few relevant features, too much regularization (a later topic).
  • Overfitting causes: model too complex relative to the amount of data, too many features, training for too long on too little data.

§5Common Mistakes

MistakeWhat HappensFix
Judging a model only by training scoreOverfitting goes unnoticedAlways compare train vs. test score together
Assuming more complexity always helpsRisk of overfitting increasesIncrease complexity only if test performance actually improves too
Practice
  1. Given these train/test score pairs, label each as underfitting, overfitting, or a good fit: (0.55, 0.53), (0.98, 0.61), (0.89, 0.86).
  2. In one sentence, explain which pair you'd be most comfortable deploying, and why.
Key takeaways
  • Underfitting: poor on both train and test. Overfitting: great on train, worse on test.
  • A good fit shows high and roughly similar scores on both.
  • Model complexity is a dial, not a one-way "more is better" setting.
